Preventive maintenance, on a schedule instead of a shoulder

The difference between a truck that runs and one that breaks down on the interstate is a schedule. Here is the practical preventive-maintenance routine — daily, weekly, by mileage and annually — that catches problems in the yard, where they are cheap.

Maintenance done to a schedule is a predictable cost. Maintenance done when something breaks is a breakdown — a tow, a lost load, a repair at a strange shop's mercy, and downtime on top of the bill. The whole game is moving the work from the shoulder to the schedule, where it is cheaper and on your terms.

Why a schedule beats reacting

The maintenance guide shows the money: a $2,400 breakdown is a $5,000-plus event once downtime and the lost load are counted. A scheduled service that catches the same failing part in the yard is a fraction of that. Prevention is not about being tidy; it is about not paying the breakdown multiplier.

You will spend the maintenance money either way. On a schedule it is a planned cost; on the shoulder it is that cost plus a tow, a lost load and a bad day.

Daily: the pre-trip

The pre-trip inspection is a legal requirement and the cheapest maintenance you do. Every day, before you roll:

  • Tyres — pressure and condition (see tires)
  • Brakes and air — leaks, adjustment, drain the tanks
  • Lights, wipers, mirrors, horn
  • Fluids — oil, coolant, DEF, leaks under the truck
  • Coupling — fifth wheel, kingpin, air and electrical lines

A defect caught here is maintenance; the same defect at a scale is a violation, and on the road it is a breakdown.

Weekly and fuel-stop checks

At fuel stops and weekly: a proper walk-around, oil level, coolant, belts and hoses for cracks, tyre pressures across the set, and a look for anything new — a fresh leak, a rubbing line, uneven wear. Five minutes that heads off the failure building quietly between services.

Mileage-based services

Follow the manufacturer's intervals, and diary them

Oil and filter changes, greasing, transmission and differential service, coolant, air dryer, fuel filters, DPF cleaning — each has a recommended interval. Put them on a mileage schedule and do them on time, not when you remember. A skipped oil change to save a day is how an engine bill arrives early. Keep a simple log of what was done at what mileage.

The annual DOT inspection

Every commercial vehicle needs a periodic (annual) DOT inspection by a qualified inspector, and the record must be kept and available — it is checked at the roadside and at your safety audit. Time it so it never lapses; an expired annual is a violation independent of the truck's actual condition.

Records: the part that proves it

Keep maintenance records — what was done, when, at what mileage, by whom. They prove compliance at an audit, they raise the truck's resale value, they help diagnose a recurring problem, and they are how you actually know your maintenance cost per mile. A truck with a documented history is worth more and easier to run than one with a shoebox and a memory.

The schedule and the reserve together

A schedule tells you when to spend; the reserve makes sure the money is there. Run both: set aside maintenance money per mile every week, and spend it against a schedule. That is the whole system, and it is the difference between maintenance being a managed line and being a series of emergencies.

Plan the spend, and reserve for it

A schedule plus a per-mile reserve turns maintenance from ambush into arithmetic. Put both in your numbers.
