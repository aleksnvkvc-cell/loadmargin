Tax, and the money that was never yours

The settlement arrives whole. A quarter to a third of the profit in it belongs to someone else, and nobody takes it out for you.

Nobody withholds anything from an owner-operator's settlement. The money arrives whole, and a large part of it was never yours. The drivers who get caught by this are not careless — they simply spent a year being paid gross for the first time in their lives.

The bill nobody warns you about

As a company driver, tax left before you saw it. As an owner-operator every dollar lands in your account and stays there until the IRS asks for it — up to fifteen months later, in one number.

Two taxes apply to the same profit:

  • Self-employment tax — Social Security and Medicare, both halves
  • Federal income tax — at your bracket, plus state where applicable

A working set-aside is 25–30% of net profit, moved to a separate account the week the money arrives. Not calculated at year end. Moved.

The single most common financial failure among new owner-operators is not a bad rate or a blown engine. It is spending money that was always going to be somebody else's.

Self-employment tax, the part that surprises

An employee pays 7.65% toward Social Security and Medicare, and the employer pays a matching 7.65%. Self-employed, you are both — so the rate is 15.3%: 12.4% Social Security up to the annual wage base, plus 2.9% Medicare with no ceiling.

Two mitigations built into the rules:

  • It applies to roughly 92.35% of net profit, not 100%.
  • Half of it is deductible against income tax.

On $70,000 of net profit that is about $9,890 in self-employment tax before any income tax at all. That is the number that shocks people, and it is entirely predictable.

Quarterly estimates, and the penalty for skipping them

The US tax system is pay-as-you-go. Self-employed people meet that through quarterly estimated payments, and skipping them creates an underpayment penalty even if you pay the full amount in April.

Quarter coversPayment normally due
January – March15 April
April – May15 June
June – August15 September
September – December15 January, following year

Note the quarters are not equal — the second one is two months. Dates shift when they fall on a weekend or holiday; confirm each year rather than assuming.

The safe-harbour rule worth knowing

You generally avoid the underpayment penalty by paying either 90% of this year's tax or 100% of last year's (110% at higher incomes) — whichever is smaller. In a first strong year, paying last year's figure is usually the cheaper and simpler route, and it removes the guessing.

Per diem: the largest deduction most drivers underclaim

If you are away from home overnight and need rest, you may deduct a standard meal and incidental allowance for each day — without keeping a single restaurant receipt. You need proof of the days, not of the meals, and your ELD already produces that.

The special transportation-industry rate, effective 1 October 2024:

Full dayPartial day (75%)
Continental US$80$60
Outside the continental US$86$64.50

DOT-regulated drivers may deduct 80% of that, rather than the 50% that applies to most business meals — a rule that exists specifically because of hours-of-service constraints.

What it is worth

280 nights away at $80 = $22,400, of which 80% is $17,920 deductible.

At a combined self-employment and income tax burden of roughly 30%, that is about $5,376 of tax you do not pay — for keeping a record you already generate.

Partial days count at 75%, so the day you leave and the day you get home are $60 each, not zero. Drivers routinely forget both ends of every trip.

Two conditions must hold: you must be away from your tax home substantially longer than an ordinary work day, and you must need sleep or rest while away. Keep the ELD records for at least three years — time, date and location is exactly what substantiates it.

⚠ The rate changes each 1 October. Confirm the current figure before filing rather than reusing last year's.

What is not

  • Commuting from home to where the truck is parked.
  • Everyday clothing, even if you only wear it working. Steel-toed boots and high-vis are deductible; jeans are not.
  • Meals when you are not away overnight. A day trip does not qualify.
  • Income lost on a deadhead or a cancelled load. You cannot deduct revenue you never earned — the cost of running those miles is already deductible.
  • Time you spent. Your own labour is not an expense; the profit is your pay.
  • The full phone bill if you also use it personally. Apportion it honestly.

The truck: depreciation and Section 179

A truck is a capital asset, normally written off over three years for a tractor and five for a trailer. Two provisions let you take much more up front:

  • Section 179 — expense a large share of qualifying equipment in the year you place it in service, subject to annual limits and to your business income.
  • Bonus depreciation — an additional first-year allowance, with the percentage set by legislation and changing between years.

Bigger is not automatically better

Writing off the whole truck in year one feels like a win and can be a mistake. It leaves nothing to deduct in years two and three, which is exactly when a new authority's income rises. You may pay less tax now and more in total.

Also: a large deduction lowers your reported income, which affects mortgage applications and truck financing for years afterwards. This is a conversation to have with an accountant before December, not in April.

Sole proprietor, LLC or S-corp

FilingSchedule CSchedule C by defaultSeparate return + payroll

Liability separationNoneYesYes

Worth considering atAny incomeAny incomeHigher, sustained profit

The S-corp election is where the real saving sits, because self-employment tax then applies only to the salary you pay yourself rather than to all profit. It also brings payroll, a separate return, an accountant, and a requirement that the salary be reasonable — the IRS challenges artificially low ones.

The saving only exceeds the added cost above a certain profit level, and where that line falls depends on your numbers. This is the clearest case in the whole guide for paying an accountant who works with owner-operators — one conversation usually pays for itself several times.

Bookkeeping that takes ten minutes a week

  1. A separate business bank account and card. Nothing else on this list works without it. Mixing personal and business spending is what turns bookkeeping into archaeology.
  2. Photograph every receipt at the moment you get it. Fuel, repairs, scales, lumpers. A photo satisfies the IRS; a faded thermal receipt in a glovebox does not.
  3. Log nights away, every trip. This is the per diem record. Your ELD has it — make sure it is retained.
  4. Move the tax set-aside weekly. The same day the settlement lands, 25–30% goes to a separate account. Treat it as already spent.
  5. Reconcile monthly, not annually. Twenty minutes a month against twelve months of guessing in April.
  6. Keep everything three years minimum, seven if you want to be comfortable.

Where tax belongs in your cost per mile

It does not go in your cost per mile — and understanding why keeps the model honest.

Cost per mile measures what it costs to run the truck. Income tax is charged on the profit that remains after those costs, so putting it in the per-mile figure double-counts and makes every load look worse than it is.

net = rate − operating costs

tax = net × set-aside rate

actually yours = net − tax

So the calculator's margin is pre-tax, deliberately. If you want the after-tax view of a target, divide: at a 28% set-aside, a $500 after-tax week needs 500 ÷ 0.72 = $694 of net profit.

The exception is employer payroll taxes if you have employees. Those are a real cost of running the truck and belong in the driver line — see the driver pay guide.

Know the pre-tax number first

The calculator gives you net profit before tax. Apply your own set-aside to that and you have the figure that actually reaches you.
