After a crash, the sequence that protects you

An accident is chaos, and what you do in the first hour shapes the claim, the liability and your record for years. Here is the calm sequence — safety, documentation, the required testing, and the reporting — worth knowing before you ever need it.

Nobody plans to crash, and that is exactly why the sequence matters — in the shock of the moment you fall back on what you already decided to do. Handled well, an accident is a managed claim. Handled badly, it is admitted liability, a missed federal drug test, and a mess that follows you for years.

First: people, then everything else

  1. Stop and secure the scene. Hazards on, and if safe, warning triangles out.
  2. Check for injuries — yours and others'. Call 911 for any injury.
  3. Do not move seriously injured people unless there is fire or immediate danger.
  4. Get to safety if the vehicle is in a live lane and it is safe to move.

People come before the truck, the load and the paperwork, always. Everything else can be reconstructed; a life cannot.

Documenting the scene

Photograph everything, while it is there

When it is safe: photograph all vehicles, positions, damage, the road, skid marks, signs, weather, and any injuries visible. Get the other driver's licence, insurance and plate; get witness names and numbers before they leave. Note the time, location and conditions. This evidence disappears in minutes and decides the claim in months.

What to say, and what not to

Be calm and cooperative with police and emergency services. But do not admit fault or speculate about blame at the scene — not to the other driver, not on a recorded call. State the facts of what happened; leave the conclusions to the investigation and the insurers. “I'm sorry” at a scene can become an admission later. Facts, not fault.

The post-accident drug and alcohol test

This is the one many drivers do not know and it is federally required. After a qualifying accident — broadly, one involving a fatality, or an injury treated away from the scene or a vehicle towed away combined with a citation to the driver — you must take a DOT post-accident drug and alcohol test within the required time windows. Missing it is treated like a refusal, which is a violation in its own right. Know your carrier's or your consortium's procedure before you need it — see the Clearinghouse guide.

Reporting and the accident register

  • Notify your insurer promptly — delay can jeopardise the claim.
  • Notify the broker/shipper if the load is affected.
  • Record it in your accident register — the record FMCSA requires you to keep, checked at the safety audit.
  • Keep every document — police report number, photos, the other party's details.

How it follows you

A DOT-recordable crash goes on your safety record and can affect your CSA and your insurance for years — and a not-at-fault crash still appears, which is one reason the documentation matters, and why the DataQs process exists for genuine errors. Handle the scene as if it will be examined, because it will be.

Prepare before it happens

Keep an accident kit and a printed procedure card in the truck: the steps above, your insurer's claim number, your post-accident testing instructions, and a checklist for the scene. In the shock of a real crash, a card you can hand yourself is worth more than a plan you meant to memorise.

A crash is the costliest event there is

Preparation and documentation are the cheapest insurance on the most expensive day. Have the procedure before you need it.
