Hotshot trucking, and whether the math works

Hotshot looks like the cheap way into trucking — a pickup and a trailer instead of a $100k semi. The entry cost is genuinely lower. Whether it earns more per year is a different question, and the honest answer is: often not.

Hotshot trucking — a heavy-duty pickup pulling a gooseneck or flatbed trailer — is marketed as the affordable way to start hauling freight. The entry cost really is lower than a Class 8 semi. But lower entry cost is not the same as higher income, and the difference is where a lot of hotshot dreams meet the arithmetic.

What hotshot actually is

A one-ton dually (a 3500-class pickup) pulling a gooseneck or bumper-pull trailer, hauling smaller loads — often flatbed-style freight like steel, equipment, machinery and building materials that does not fill a full semi. It sits between a cargo van and a full tractor-trailer.

The real appeal: entry cost

The honest advantage is capital. A used dually and a gooseneck is a fraction of the price of a sleeper tractor and a trailer, so the debt and the insurance are lower, and the barrier to starting is real for someone without $80,000–$150,000 for a Class 8 setup.

Hotshot lowers the cost to start. It does not lower your cost per mile, and it lowers the revenue you can earn per mile of driving. Those two facts decide everything.

The rules still apply

A common misunderstanding: hotshot is not a regulatory loophole. If you are for-hire and interstate, and especially over 10,001 lb combined, most of the same rules apply — USDOT number, operating authority, insurance, and often a CDL depending on the combined weight rating.

The CDL line

A combination rated over 26,001 lb generally requires a CDL, and many real hotshot setups cross that line once you count the trailer's rating. Below it you may avoid the CDL but you also cap your payload hard. Either way you still need authority, insurance, IFTA, UCR, ELD where applicable, and the rest — the compliance cost is not much smaller than a semi's.

The payload ceiling

The hard limit is what you can legally and safely carry. A hotshot rig hauls a few thousand to maybe ten-plus thousand pounds — far less than a semi's ~45,000 lb of payload. That ceiling means:

  • You cannot take the heavy, high-paying full loads a semi can
  • You compete for smaller, partial and expedited freight
  • Your revenue per trip is capped by the trailer, not by the market

Rates, and why they run lower

Hotshot freight generally pays less per mile than heavy flatbed on a semi, because the loads are lighter and the barrier to entry is lower — more people can buy a used dually than a semi, so more compete. The rate can spike for genuinely expedited or specialised runs, but the steady-state rate is below what a Class 8 flatbed earns.

The costs people forget

Hotshot economics get oversold because the costs get undercounted:

  • Pickups wear out doing this. A one-ton truck run hard at freight miles depreciates and needs work — it was not built for a million miles like a Class 8 engine.
  • Fuel economy is not dramatically better once you are loaded and fighting wind with a gooseneck.
  • Insurance and authority cost nearly the same as a small semi operation.
  • Deadhead hurts more when your loaded revenue per mile is already lower.

Run your cost per mile honestly and the gap between the low entry cost and the modest net becomes visible fast.

The honest verdict

Hotshot is a legitimate way in for someone who has the truck already, wants lower startup risk, or likes the lighter, more local flatbed niche. It is not a shortcut to more money than a used semi — the lower rates and the payload ceiling usually mean lower annual net, not higher.

If the goal is to earn the most, a used Class 8 flatbed generally out-earns a hotshot once both are running. If the goal is to start with less capital and less risk, hotshot has a real place. Choose on which of those goals is actually yours, on your own numbers — not on the low sticker price.
