How to calculate your true cost per mile

The single number every freight decision depends on, and the one most carriers get wrong. Worked through end to end with real arithmetic.

Ask ten carriers what a mile costs them and you get ten confident answers. Most are wrong by fifteen to twenty-five cents. On a 900-mile load that is between $135 and $225 of margin that exists on paper and not in the bank account. This guide fixes the number.

Why the number is usually wrong

Cost per mile is not hard arithmetic. It is hard bookkeeping. The division itself takes ten seconds; assembling the two figures you divide takes an afternoon, and it is an afternoon that never has a deadline attached to it.

So the industry substitutes something easier: rate per mile. A broker says $2.65 a mile, a dispatcher hears "that's decent", and the load is booked. Rate per mile is quick to say and it is wrong often enough to be dangerous, because it silently assumes two things that are almost never true — that all miles are loaded, and that your costs match the carrier next to you.

The consequence shows up in the industry-wide numbers rather than in any one carrier's books. In its 2026 Operational Costs of Trucking analysis, the American Transportation Research Institute put average operating margins in the truckload and refrigerated sectors below 1.0% for 2025, with flatbed carriers at −0.5% — an operating loss for the sector as a whole.[1] At a margin that thin, a twenty-cent error in cost per mile is not a rounding issue. It is the difference between the year working and not working.

A load does not become profitable because the rate per mile sounded good. It becomes profitable when the rate exceeds every cost the load causes.

What actually goes into it

Costs split into two groups, and the split matters more than the list.

TypeBehaviourExamples
Fixed Charged whether the truck moves or not. Spread across whatever miles you actually run. Truck and trailer payments, insurance, permits, plates, IFTA, ELD subscription, parking, accounting, phone
Variable Incurred per mile driven. Runs whether the trailer is loaded or empty. Fuel, tyres, maintenance and repair, driver pay per mile, tolls

Fixed cost is where the error lives. Fuel is visible — you watch the pump. A truck payment is invisible on any given load, because it already left the account on the first of the month whether you ran 12,000 miles or 6,000.

Fixed cost: the part everyone forgets

Add up everything that bills monthly regardless of movement. A single-truck operation typically looks something like this:

ItemPer monthNotes
Truck payment$2,450Or depreciation, if owned outright
Trailer payment$620
Insurance$1,350Liability, cargo, physical damage, occupational accident
Permits, plates, IFTA$310Annual costs divided by twelve
ELD, software, phone$185
Parking, admin, accounting$540Everything that keeps the business legal and organised
Total fixed$5,455Before a wheel turns

These are illustrative figures for a typical single-truck operation, not a benchmark. Replace every line with yours — the whole point of this exercise is that your number is not the industry's number.

If you own the truck outright

Do not write zero. A paid-off truck still consumes value every mile, and one day it needs replacing. Use a replacement reserve: expected replacement cost divided by the months you expect to keep running it. A $140,000 truck you plan to replace in five years is roughly $2,330 a month, whether or not a lender is asking for it.

Carriers who skip this line feel wealthy for four years and then cannot afford the truck they need in year five.

Variable cost: the part that moves

Three of these are straightforward and one is routinely underestimated.

Fuel

Model it in gallons, never in cents per mile:

fuel_per_mile = diesel_price ÷ mpg

At $3.85 diesel and 6.5 mpg that is $0.592 per mile. The reason to keep price and efficiency separate is diagnostic: if your fuel cost per mile rises, gallons tell you instantly whether the market moved or the truck did. A single blended number hides both causes, and they have completely different fixes.

Maintenance, repair and tyres

Take twelve months of every invoice — oil changes, brakes, tyres, the alternator that failed in Amarillo — and divide by the miles run in the same period. Most single-truck operations land between $0.15 and $0.25 a mile. ATRI reported repair and maintenance costs rising 8.6% and tyres 6.4% in 2025, so a figure you calculated two years ago is already optimistic.[1]

Driver pay

If you pay per mile, it is a variable cost and it belongs here. If you pay a percentage of the line haul, it is not per-mile at all and must be modelled against revenue instead — which changes the arithmetic on every load. If you drive yourself, either pay yourself a wage here and treat the remainder as business profit, or take zero here and treat all of it as your income. Both are defensible. Mixing them is not.

Tolls

Lane-specific rather than fleet-wide, so most carriers charge them to the individual load rather than into cost per mile. Either is fine as long as you do not do both. ATRI recorded tolls rising 13.2% in 2025 — the fastest-rising line item in the study.[1]

Working it out, step by step

Two numbers, one division.

fixed_cost_per_mile = total_monthly_fixed ÷ monthly_miles

Using the table above and a realistic 10,500 miles a month:

Monthly fixed cost$5,455
Miles driven per month10,500
Fixed cost per mile$0.520

Now stack the variable costs on top to get the all-in figure — the number below which a mile loses money:

ComponentPer mile
Fixed cost$0.520
Fuel ($3.85 ÷ 6.5 mpg)$0.592
Maintenance and tyres$0.190
Driver pay$0.620
All-in break-even$1.922

That $1.92 is the honest floor. Every mile the truck turns — loaded or empty — costs that much before a cent of profit exists.

The mistake that ruins the whole calculation

Everything above hangs on one figure: monthly miles. Get it wrong and every number downstream is wrong in the same direction.

The universal error is using the miles you hope to run instead of the miles you actually run. Look at the arithmetic:

Monthly milesFixed cost per mileEffect
12,000 (a good month)$0.455The number you want to believe
10,500 (realistic average)$0.520The number to use
9,000 (a slow month)$0.606What actually happened in February

Fifteen cents separates the optimistic figure from the pessimistic one, and the truck does not care which you wrote down. Use a twelve-month average including the bad months, holidays and the week the truck sat in the shop.

A slow month hurts twice: less revenue comes in, and every mile you did run became more expensive.

How your number compares

ATRI's 2026 report put the industry average marginal cost at $2.336 per mile for 2025, or $1.854 excluding fuel.[1] That figure covers fleets of every size, including large carriers with purchasing power an owner-operator does not have.

Use it as a sanity check, not as a target:

  • Far below it? Something is missing. The usual suspects are a paid-off truck with no replacement reserve, unpaid owner labour, or maintenance averaged over a year with no major repair in it.
  • Far above it? Worth investigating, but not automatically wrong. Specialised equipment, high-cost insurance states and low annual mileage all legitimately push it up.
  • Close to it? That tells you your bookkeeping is plausible. It does not tell you your rates are.

Using it on a real load

A broker offers $2,400 for 850 loaded miles, with 120 miles of deadhead to reach the pickup.

Rate per mile says $2.82 and sounds excellent. Here is the real arithmetic:

Line haul revenue$2,400
Total miles (850 + 120)970
Fuel — 149 gal at $3.85−$575
Driver pay — 970 × $0.62−$601
Maintenance — 970 × $0.19−$184
Fixed cost — 970 × $0.52−$504
Tolls and fees−$85
Net profit$451
Margin18.8%
All-in rate per mile$2.47
Break-even rate per mile$1.98

The load is genuinely good — but notice that the honest rate per mile is $2.47, not $2.82. The 120 deadhead miles took 35 cents off the top before anything else happened. That gap is where most thin loads hide.

The same arithmetic works in reverse when a broker lowballs you. If your total cost is $1,949 and you want a 15% margin, the rate you need is:

target_rate = total_cost ÷ (1 − target_margin) = 1,949 ÷ 0.85 = $2,293

That is a specific number you can say out loud on the phone, which is worth considerably more than a feeling that the rate is too low.

When to update it

Cost per mile is not an annual exercise. Recalculate whenever any of these happen:

  • Insurance renews. The single largest fixed line, and it moves.
  • A truck comes off warranty. Maintenance per mile steps up, and it never steps back down.
  • Driver pay changes. Obvious, and still routinely forgotten in the cost model.
  • Monthly miles shift. Losing a dedicated lane changes the denominator, which changes every mile you run.
  • You add or park a truck. Fixed costs rarely scale in a straight line.

A quarterly review takes twenty minutes once the first calculation exists. The first one is the only hard one.

Six mistakes that cost real money

  1. Using loaded miles only. Deadhead burns the same fuel and carries the same fixed cost. Divide by total miles or every figure is optimistic. More on this here.
  2. Forgetting the owner's wage. If you drive, your labour is a cost. Leaving it at zero makes a marginal operation look profitable and hides the fact that you are working for free.
  3. Averaging maintenance over a good year. Twelve months with no major failure is not a representative twelve months. Use a longer window if you have one.
  4. Ignoring detention. A truck sitting at a dock still carries fixed cost by the hour. Four unpaid hours on a $0.52-per-mile truck running 500 miles a day is roughly $98 of cost nobody invoiced for.
  5. Using national average diesel. Use what you actually pay, including the difference between your best fuel stop and the one you were forced into at 2am.
  6. Setting it once and never revisiting. The 2024 number is comfortably wrong in 2026. ATRI's data shows costs rose 3.4% in a single year.[1]
