How to structure a carrier, without the myths

Sole proprietor, LLC, S-corp — the choice affects your liability, your taxes and how a broker sees you. Here is what each actually does, when the S-corp election starts to pay, and the setup that has to be in place before your first load.

Business structure is one of the few decisions that touches liability, taxes and credibility at once. It is not complicated, but it is surrounded by bad advice — both the people who think an LLC is a magic shield and the people who think it does nothing. Here is the honest middle.

Why structure matters for a carrier specifically

Trucking carries real liability — a loaded truck can cause a serious accident, and cargo claims run into real money. It is also a business where a broker or shipper decides whether to work with you partly on how established you look. Structure affects both the risk you carry personally and the face you present.

Two questions decide this: what happens to your house if the truck is in a bad accident, and how much are you paying in self-employment tax. Structure is how you answer both.

Sole proprietor: the default you did not choose

Do nothing, and you are a sole proprietor. It is free and simple — you and the business are the same legal person. That is also the problem: there is no separation. A judgment against the business is a judgment against you, and your personal assets are exposed.

For a one-truck operation just testing the water it can be a starting point, but the moment there is a real truck, real insurance and real freight, the exposure is the reason almost nobody stays here.

The LLC: what it does and does not do

An LLC makes the business a separate legal entity. Done properly, it means a business liability generally stops at the business — your personal assets sit behind a wall.

What an LLC does NOT do

It does not replace insurance — it sits behind it, and insurance pays first. It does not protect you from your own negligence behind the wheel in every case. And it does not survive if you ignore the formalities (see “piercing the veil” below). An LLC is a wall; a wall with holes in it is not a wall.

By default a single-member LLC is taxed exactly like a sole proprietor — the liability changes, the tax does not, until you make an election.

The S-corp election, and when it starts to pay

An LLC can elect to be taxed as an S-corporation. This is where the tax saving lives, and it is worth understanding the mechanism rather than the slogan.

As a sole proprietor or default LLC, all your profit is subject to self-employment tax (about 15.3%). As an S-corp, you pay yourself a reasonable salary — which carries payroll tax — and take the rest as a distribution that does not. The self-employment tax on the distribution portion goes away.

The S-corp saves the self-employment tax on the profit above a reasonable salary. Below a certain profit that saving is smaller than the cost of running it — above it, the election pays for itself several times over.

The catch: an S-corp means real payroll, a real salary you cannot set to zero, more accounting, and a stricter paper trail. There is a break-even profit below which it is not worth the overhead and above which it clearly is. That break-even is exactly the conversation to have with an accountant — it depends on your numbers, and getting the “reasonable salary” wrong invites an IRS problem.

Why not a C-corp

A C-corporation is taxed at the entity level and then again on distributions — double taxation — which is why owner-operators almost never use one. It exists for businesses raising outside investment, not for a carrier hauling freight. Skip it.

EIN, banking and the paper trail

Whatever you choose, you need:

  • An EIN from the IRS — free, and required for Form 2290, for hiring, and for a business bank account. Get it early; a new EIN takes about two weeks to become active.
  • A separate business bank account, used for every business dollar in and out
  • A separate card, and clean books — see the banking and bookkeeping guide

Keeping the protection real

An LLC only protects you if you treat it as a real separate business. Mix personal and business money, pay your mortgage out of the business account, keep no records, and a court can “pierce the veil” — treat the LLC as a sham and expose you personally anyway. The protection is not the paperwork; it is the discipline behind it:

  • Business money stays in the business account
  • You pay yourself deliberately, not by dipping in
  • Contracts are signed in the business name
  • The annual state filing is kept current

How to actually decide

  1. Almost every carrier: form an LLC. The liability separation alone justifies it the moment there is a truck and insurance.
  2. Stay default-taxed while profit is modest — the S-corp overhead is not worth it yet.
  3. Revisit the S-corp election with an accountant once profit is consistently into the range where the self-employment saving beats the payroll and accounting cost.
  4. Keep the wall real with separate banking and clean books from day one.

None of this is legal or tax advice, and the right answer depends on your state and your numbers. But the shape is stable: LLC for the liability, S-corp election later for the tax, discipline throughout so the protection actually holds.

Structure is fixed overhead too

State filing fees, the registered agent, the extra accounting on an S-corp — all of it is overhead that belongs in your cost per mile, not a surprise in April.
