Quick pay vs factoring, getting paid before day 30

Waiting 30-plus days for a broker to pay strangles a new carrier's cash. Quick pay and factoring both fix it, for a fee. They are not the same, they do not cost the same, and the right one depends on your situation — here is how to choose.

A broker paying in 30 days is normal; a new carrier surviving 30 days with no cash coming in is hard. Both quick pay and factoring get money to you faster, for a fee. They work differently and cost differently, and the “cheaper” one depends entirely on your cash-flow reality, not on which percentage is smaller.

The problem: the gap before payment

You fuel, you drive, you deliver — and then you wait weeks to be paid, while the next load's fuel and this week's truck payment are due now. That gap is the working-capital problem that sinks new carriers with profitable loads but no cash. Quick pay and factoring both buy down the wait; the question is at what price.

The issue is rarely whether the loads are profitable. It is whether you can survive the weeks between hauling them and being paid for them.

Quick pay: the broker's own offer

Quick pay is the broker paying you early — say in a few days instead of 30 — in exchange for a small discount off the invoice. It is per-broker, optional, and you choose it load by load. There is no contract and no third party; you just accept a slightly smaller amount to be paid now.

  • Pro: simple, no commitment, you pick when to use it
  • Con: only available from that broker, terms vary, and a per-load discount adds up if you use it on everything

Factoring: a third party buys the invoice

Factoring is a company that buys your invoices and pays you most of the value immediately, then collects from the broker. It covers all your invoices through one relationship, and often bundles credit checks on brokers and back-office help. It is a contract, with a fee per invoice, and sometimes minimums or recourse terms. See the factoring guide for the full cost-of-capital math — a 3% fee against a 30-day payer is a high annualised cost, even when it is the right call.

When each makes sense

Match the tool to the pattern, not the fee

Quick pay suits a carrier who is mostly fine on cash but hits the occasional gap — use it selectively, on the loads and weeks you need it, and keep the full rate the rest of the time. Factoring suits a carrier who needs every invoice paid fast and steady — a new authority with no cushion, or one growing faster than its cash. The mistake is defaulting to factoring everything when occasional quick pay would cost far less overall.

When you should use neither

Both are a cost of capital — money you give up to be paid sooner. The goal is to outgrow needing them: as you build a cash cushion and business credit, you can carry the 30-day gap yourself and keep the whole rate. Some carriers factor forever out of habit long after they could self-fund. Treat quick pay and factoring as a bridge to build across, not a permanent tax on every load — and always know, in cents per mile, what the convenience is costing.

Know the cost of getting paid early

Quick pay and factoring both cost real money per load. Put the fee in your number and use them deliberately, not by default.
