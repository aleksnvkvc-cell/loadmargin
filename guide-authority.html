What authority really costs

The FMCSA fee is $300. The first year is twelve to forty thousand. Here is every line of the difference, and the cash-flow trough that is not on anybody's list.

Guide Updated 26 August 2026 12 min read

Getting your own authority costs a few hundred dollars in government fees and somewhere between twelve and forty thousand in everything else. The gap between those two numbers is where new carriers fail, and almost all of it is insurance.

The question behind the question

"How much does authority cost" is nearly always asked when the real question is can I afford to run under my own authority instead of leasing on. Those are different calculations, and the second one is answered at the end of this guide.

The registration itself is cheap and mostly a paperwork exercise. What you are really buying is the obligation to carry your own insurance, chase your own money, and cover your own gaps — and those are priced by a market that treats a carrier with no history as exactly what it is.

The fees, itemised

Current published figures for a single-truck carrier registering in 2026:

ItemCostFrequency
USDOT number$0Once — registration itself is free
MC operating authority (FMCSA filing fee)$300Once, non-refundable, per authority type
BOC-3 process agent filing$25 – $50Once, nationwide
UCR registration (0–2 vehicles)$69 – $73Annual
IRP apportioned plates$500 – $3,000+Annual, scales with states operated
IFTA licence and decals$10 – $50Initial setup, per base state
HVUT — Form 2290 heavy vehicle use tax$100 – $550Annual, per vehicle, by gross weight
ELD hardware and subscription$180 – $480Annual
Drug & alcohol consortium enrolment$150 – $300Annual
Government and compliance subtotal$1,334 – $4,803

Note the $300. That is the entire FMCSA charge for operating authority, and it is the number that filing services quote alongside their own fee of several hundred more. Paying a service is a reasonable choice if you value not making a mistake on the form; paying one because you believe the authority itself costs $900 is not.

The USDOT number is free

Registration for a USDOT number carries no FMCSA fee at all. Any quote that prices it as a government cost is pricing their own labour. Both the USDOT number and the MC authority can be applied for directly on the FMCSA registration system.

Insurance is the whole decision

Everything above is rounding. Insurance is typically 70–80% of first-year startup spend, and it is the line that decides whether the plan works.

CoverageYear oneNotes
Primary liability ($750k–$1M)$10,000 – $25,000Required. The federal minimum for general freight is $750,000
Cargo ($100k typical)$1,500 – $3,000Required in practice by nearly every broker
Physical damage$2,500 – $6,000Required if the truck is financed
Insurance subtotal$14,000 – $34,000

Three things move that range more than anything else you control:

  1. Authority age. Under twelve months you are in the highest bracket that exists. This is not negotiable and it is not personal — it is the loss data. It improves sharply at the one-year and two-year marks if you stay clean.
  2. Driving record and CDL age. An experienced driver with a clean MVR and a new authority pays materially less than a new CDL with a new authority.
  3. Radius and lanes. Regional operation is cheaper than coast to coast. Certain metros and certain commodities carry surcharges that dwarf any saving you make elsewhere.
Down payments matter as much as the annual premium. Many new-authority policies require 20–25% down, which means a $22,000 policy needs $5,000 in cash before the truck moves.

Three realistic totals

First-year all-in, excluding the truck itself:

ScenarioFirst yearWho this is
Favourable≈ $12,800Clean record, experienced driver, paid-off truck, regional lanes
Typical≈ $24,500Most new single-truck carriers
Difficult≈ $38,800New CDL or blemished record, financed truck, coast-to-coast

Add the truck. A used sleeper in serviceable condition and a trailer will move the total well past any of those figures, and a financed truck adds the physical damage premium above plus a payment that starts before the first invoice is paid.

The number nobody budgets: working capital

This is what actually kills new authorities, and it never appears on a startup-cost list because it is not a cost. It is a timing problem.

You pay for fuel on the day you buy it. You get paid for the load 30 to 45 days after you invoice, and only if the paperwork is clean. Between those two events you are financing the freight yourself.

WeekCash outCash inRunning balance
1−$2,150$0−$2,150
2−$2,150$0−$4,300

The trough is $8,600, in week four, before a single dollar arrives. That figure is your working capital requirement and it is separate from every number above. Underestimating it is why profitable new carriers fail: the operation works and the bank account does not survive long enough to prove it.

Two ways to shrink the trough

Factoring converts the 30–45 day wait into 24 hours for 1–4% of the invoice. On the schedule above that removes almost the entire trough. Whether it is worth it is arithmetic, not ideology — see the factoring guide.

Quick pay from the broker does the same thing at a similar discount, usually 2–5%, without a contract. Cheaper per use, unavailable when you need it most.

What you have to earn to stand still

Take the typical scenario: $24,500 of first-year fixed and compliance cost, plus a truck payment. Assume the truck runs 100,000 loaded miles in the year.

Startup, compliance and insurance, year one$24,500
Truck and trailer payments, 12 months$21,600
Other fixed overhead (permits, accounting, phone, parking)$4,800
Total annual fixed$50,900
÷ 100,000 miles$0.509 fixed cost per mile

That half-dollar sits underneath every load you will ever price, before fuel, before the driver, before a single wrench is turned. Add variable cost of roughly $1.40 and your break-even is around $1.91 per total mile — and total means including deadhead.

At 12% deadhead, $1.91 per total mile is $2.17 per loaded mile just to break even. That is the number to hold in your head the next time a load board shows $2.10.

Authority versus leasing on

Leasing onto an established carrier means they carry the authority, the insurance and usually the collections. You keep a percentage — commonly 65–75% of the line haul — and you avoid nearly every cost on this page.

Leased on (70%)Own authority
Gross freight billed, year$220,000$220,000
Retained$154,000$220,000
Insurance and compliance−$2,500−$24,500
Fuel, maintenance, tolls−$96,000−$96,000
Truck payments−$21,600−$21,600
Overhead and admin time−$1,200−$6,800
Net$32,700$71,100

The gap looks enormous, and on these assumptions it is. But the assumptions are doing the work: the own-authority column assumes you keep the truck as full as the carrier's dispatch did, that you collect everything you bill, and that nothing uninsured happens. Those are three real risks that the 30% was buying.

The honest test

Authority is worth it when you can keep the truck loaded yourself and survive a bad quarter. If either is uncertain, the percentage you give up is the cheapest insurance in the industry.

A sane order of operations

  1. Get insurance quotes first, before spending anything. They decide whether the plan is viable and they take the longest. Quote with the actual truck, radius and commodities.
  2. Form the business entity and get an EIN.
  3. Apply for the USDOT number and MC authority — $300, directly with FMCSA.
  4. File the BOC-3 through a process agent.
  5. Have the insurer file proof of coverage with FMCSA. Authority is not active until this lands, and the 21-day protest period runs in parallel.
  6. Register UCR, IRP and IFTA, and file Form 2290.
  7. Set up ELD and the drug & alcohol consortium before the first mile, not after the first inspection.
  8. Build the cost model and know your break-even before you look at a load board.
