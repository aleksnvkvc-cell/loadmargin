Negotiating with numbers

Moving a rate from $2,150 to $2,280 is a 5.7% change in the rate and a 75% change in the profit. That asymmetry is invisible if you think in rate per mile.

Rate negotiation is not persuasion. It is the moment where you find out whether you know your own numbers. A carrier who can state a break-even to the cent, calmly, wins most of these conversations without raising their voice.

The premise most carriers get wrong

The common instinct is to negotiate against the market: what is this lane paying. That is useful context and it is the wrong anchor, because the market rate tells you what somebody will pay, not what you can afford to accept.

The right anchor is internal. There is a rate below which the load makes you poorer than parking the truck, and there is a rate below which it makes you poorer than the next call. Both are arithmetic and neither depends on what the broker says.

You are not negotiating over a price. You are deciding whether a specific piece of capacity, on a specific day, is better spent on this load or on the alternative. The price is just where that decision gets expressed.

Knowing your walk-away number

Three numbers, computed before the phone rings.

Variable break-evenFuel + driver + maintenance + tolls, on total milesAbsolute floor. Below this the load costs you cash to run

Target rateFull cost ÷ (1 − target margin)What you are actually asking for

Why the target is a division and not an addition

Adding 18% to cost gives $2,333.38, and the margin on that is 15.3%, not 18%. Margin is measured against revenue, so you divide by (1 − margin). Every carrier who "adds 20%" is quietly running at about 16.7%.

Building the counter

The broker offers $2,150. Your target is $2,412 and your full break-even is $1,977. You have $435 of room between the offer and the floor, and $262 between the offer and the target.

Ask above the target, not at it. Not to be greedy — because the counter is the anchor and the settlement usually lands between. Ask $2,550 and settle at $2,400 and you got your number. Ask $2,412 and settle at $2,280 and you did not.

Note how small the moves are in percentage terms and how large in dollars. Moving the offer from $2,150 to $2,280 is $130 — a 5.7% rate difference and a 75% increase in net profit. That asymmetry is the entire reason to negotiate at all, and it is invisible if you think in rate per mile.

Working backwards from a margin

The most useful habit in freight pricing is to stop asking "is this rate good" and start asking "what rate would make this a load I want".

target_rate = total_cost ÷ (1 − target_margin)

min_rate = total_cost ÷ (1 − min_margin)

Set two margins as policy, once, and then never negotiate them per load:

  • Target margin — what a normal load should earn. 15–20% is a reasonable working band for a small carrier on spot freight.
  • Minimum margin — the level below which you decline unless there is a positioning reason. 8–10% is common.

Having those written down converts a hundred judgement calls a month into a single decision made once, calmly, when nobody is waiting on the phone.

Four things to say, and one never to

1. State the number, not a feeling. "I need $2,550 on that" beats "that's a little low". The first is a position. The second is an invitation to explain why it is not.

2. Name the reason if there is one. "There's 120 miles of deadhead to your shipper, so I'm running 970 for an 850-mile rate." Brokers know deadhead is real. Most have no idea how much of it your particular truck has, because they cannot see where you are.

3. Offer a trade instead of a concession. "I can do $2,300 if you can get me a 14:00 pickup instead of 08:00" or "…if detention starts after one hour". You give price, you get something that is worth money to you. See the detention guide for what those terms are worth.

4. Be willing to say no, once, quietly. "I understand — that one doesn't work for me. Call me if it's still open this afternoon." A remarkable proportion of loads are re-offered higher. Nothing about that sentence burns a relationship.

The one to never say

"What can you do?" It concedes the anchor, it signals you have no number, and it guarantees the next figure you hear is barely above the last. If you have a break-even, say a number. If you do not have a break-even, that is the actual problem.

Negotiating with evidence

Anyone can assert that a lane is underpriced. Very few carriers can show it, and the ones who can get different answers.

Three kinds of evidence that move rates, all of which are things you already have if you record them:

  1. Your own lane history. "We've run Columbus–Charlotte eleven times this year, average all-in $2.68. At $2.31 I'd be below where I've been running it."
  2. Dwell records by facility. "Your consignee averaged 3.4 hours across our last seven visits against a two-hour free window. I'll take it at $2,400 with detention from hour two, or $2,250 with detention from hour one."
  3. Deadhead reality. "I'm in Toledo. That's 118 deadhead. On total miles your offer is $1.96 and my break-even is $2.04." A number nobody can dispute because it is about your truck, not the market.

None of this is aggressive. All of it is specific, and specificity is what separates a carrier a broker calls first from one they call when the cheap trucks are gone.

When to take the cheap load anyway

Rules that only say no are bad rules. There are four situations where a below-target load is the correct decision, and they all share the same logic: the comparison is against the real alternative, not against your target.

  1. The alternative is deadhead. If you are going to drive those miles empty regardless, any load covering more than its own variable cost beats empty. See the deadhead guide — the arithmetic is stark.
  2. It repositions you into a strong market. A thin load into Chicago on Thursday that lets you take a rich Friday load is one decision, not two. Score the pair.
  3. It fills a day that was otherwise idle. Fixed cost accrues whether or not you move. A load at 6% margin on an idle day contributes 6%; parking contributes nothing and costs the same fixed.
  4. It is a relationship you are deliberately buying. Legitimate, but name it — "I am spending $200 to get on this shipper's list" — and set a limit. Undeclared, this becomes the reason a fleet runs at 2% margin for a year.

What all four have in common is that the load is still evaluated against a number. None of them is "the broker seemed nice about it".

Contract lanes are a different game

On spot freight you are pricing one load. On a contract lane you are pricing a commitment, and three things change:

  • Fuel risk moves. A contract rate held for a year without a proper surcharge is a bet on diesel. Get the surcharge terms right — base, peg and index — before the rate. See the fuel surcharge guide.
  • The backhaul is part of the price. A committed outbound into a dead market is a committed deadhead return. Price the round trip.
  • Volume commitments cut both ways. A guaranteed two loads a week is worth a lower rate. A "we expect around" is worth nothing and should be priced as spot.

The right target margin on contract freight is usually lower than spot, and that is correct — you are being paid partly in predictability, which has real value in the fixed-cost arithmetic.

Keep the record

The single highest-return administrative habit in a small carrier: record every load with rate, loaded miles, deadhead, facility dwell and net, and review the lanes monthly.

After ninety days that record answers, with evidence, the four questions that decide a year: which lanes actually pay, which facilities cost you a day a month, which brokers pay on time, and what your real average all-in rate is per lane. Every one of those is a negotiating position you cannot fake and a competitor without records cannot match.
