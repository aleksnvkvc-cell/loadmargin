Weight, axles and the ninety-second fix

You can be 78,000 pounds gross and still be overweight. The scale does not weigh your truck — it weighs each axle group, one at a time.

Almost every overweight ticket is a distribution problem, not a loading problem. The truck is under 80,000 pounds and one axle group is over — which is fixable in about ninety seconds, if you know which way to slide and by how much.

The three federal limits

On the Interstate system, three numbers apply at once. You must satisfy all three.

WhereFederal limit
Steer axle (single)12,000 lb
Drive tandem34,000 lb
Trailer tandem34,000 lb
Gross vehicle weight80,000 lb

Note that 12,000 + 34,000 + 34,000 is 80,000 exactly. There is no slack anywhere: being legal on gross and illegal on an axle is not only possible, it is the normal way to get a ticket.

The 12,000 lb steer limit is federal. Many tractors are rated higher and many states allow more on a steer with appropriate tyres — but tyre rating governs too, and the sticker on the sidewall is a limit no permit overrides.

You can be 78,000 pounds gross and still be overweight. The scale does not weigh your truck — it weighs each axle group, one at a time.

The bridge formula, in plain English

The Federal Bridge Gross Weight Formula exists to stop concentrated weight destroying bridges. The principle is simple: the closer together a group of axles is, the less weight it may carry. Spread the same load over more distance and more axles, and you may carry more.

That is why the 34,000 lb tandem limit assumes a normal tandem spacing, and why a standard five-axle tractor-trailer at legal spacing tops out at 80,000. It is also why closing your trailer tandems all the way forward can put you out of compliance even when the axle scale reading looks acceptable.

In practice, for a standard 5-axle van or reefer running interstate, you almost never need to compute the formula. You need the three axle limits above and the 80,000 gross. The formula matters when you are running specialised configurations, multiple trailers, or short wheelbases — and there it is worth doing properly rather than approximating.

Why 80,000 lb is not always your limit

  • State roads are not federal roads. Off the Interstate, state limits apply and they differ. Some are lower.
  • Frost laws. In spring thaw, northern states cut allowable weights sharply on designated routes, sometimes by 35–50%. They are seasonal, posted, and enforced.
  • Bridges post their own limits, which override everything.
  • Your equipment has ratings. GAWR on each axle, GVWR on the vehicle, and a load rating on every tyre. The lowest applicable number wins, always.
  • Some states allow more with permits or on designated routes, and a handful have grandfathered higher limits.

The rule that keeps you out of trouble

The binding limit is the lowest of: federal, state, bridge posting, axle rating and tyre rating. Not whichever one you remembered. A permit can raise a legal limit; it cannot raise a tyre's load rating.

Sliding the tandems: the only lever you have at the scale

Once the trailer is loaded and sealed, you cannot move the freight. You can move the axles under it — and that is what fixes most weight problems.

Which way to slide

Drives heavy, trailer light → slide the trailer tandems FORWARD. Moving the trailer axles toward the nose shifts weight off the drives onto the trailer tandems.

Trailer heavy, drives light → slide the trailer tandems BACK.

Rule of thumb: each hole moves roughly 250–400 lb between the two groups, depending on the trailer. Learn your own trailer's figure once — weigh, slide one hole, weigh again — and you will never guess again.

Two things people get wrong. First, sliding the trailer tandems does not change your gross weight — it only redistributes between drives and trailer. If you are over 80,000, no amount of sliding helps and freight has to come off. Second, sliding affects your legal trailer length in some states (California's 40-foot kingpin-to-axle rule being the famous one), so the legal slide position is not always the one that balances best.

The fifth wheel, and the axle nobody thinks about

Sliding the fifth wheel moves weight between the steer and the drives — a different pair from the trailer tandems.

  • Fifth wheel forward → more weight on the steer, less on the drives
  • Fifth wheel back → less on the steer, more on the drives

Steer overweight is less common but it happens on heavy-nose loads, and it is the one drivers forget they can fix. It also matters for handling: too light on the steer is a steering and braking problem long before it is a legal one.

Set the fifth wheel once for the tractor and leave it, unless you have a specific reason. It is the coarse adjustment; the trailer tandems are the fine one.

Fuel weight, and why you scale before you top off

Diesel weighs roughly 7 lb per gallon. Two 150-gallon tanks full is about 2,100 lb — more than 2.5% of your gross limit, and it sits mostly on the steer and drives.

Fuel stateApprox. weightEffect
300 gal full2,100 lbMostly on steer and drives
150 gal half1,050 lb
Difference1,050 lbEnough to put drives over on a heavy load

The sequence that avoids a re-weigh fee

Fuel first, then scale. If you scale on half tanks and then put 150 gallons in, you have added about 1,050 lb that the ticket does not know about — and most of it landed on the drives.

If you cannot fuel first, at least know what your fuel state was when you weighed, and account for it.

Scaling properly, the first time

  1. Fuel to the level you will actually run at.
  2. Pull onto a certified scale (CAT or similar) and get all three groups — steer, drives, trailer — not just gross.
  3. Compare each group to its limit, not to 80,000.
  4. If a group is over, slide — trailer tandems for drives-vs-trailer, fifth wheel for steer-vs-drives.
  5. Re-weigh. The re-weigh at the same scale is usually a fraction of the first ticket, which is the whole reason to use the same scale.
  6. Keep the ticket. It is your evidence if a DOT scale disagrees, and it is a deductible expense.

Scale when the load is heavy, when it is unevenly distributed, when you did not watch it loaded, or when the bill of lading weight looks optimistic. A $14 scale ticket against a four-figure fine is not a close decision.

What being overweight actually costs

Fines vary enormously by state and by how far over you are — from under a hundred dollars for a small overage to several thousand, with some states charging per pound over. But the fine is rarely the biggest cost.

CostNotes
The fine itselfWide range by state; escalates steeply with the overage
Being held until legalYou may not be permitted to move. Hours or a day gone
Getting legalOff-loading, a second truck, or a permit purchased at a bad moment
The lost dayAt roughly $196 of fixed cost a day plus lost contribution — see the detention guide

InsuranceA worse CSA record shows up at renewal

That last pair is what makes an overweight ticket disproportionate. A $300 fine that helps push a BASIC over threshold can cost thousands at renewal, twelve months later, in a way nobody connects back to the scale.

Working out your real payload

Do this once for your own tractor and trailer and write it on something you keep in the cab.

Legal gross limit80,000 lb
Tractor, wet, with full fuel−18,500 lb
Trailer, empty−14,500 lb
Driver, gear, chains, straps−600 lb
Available payload46,400 lb

Those tare weights are illustrative — a day cab is lighter, a heavy-spec sleeper with an APU is heavier, a reefer is heavier than a dry van. Weigh your own empty combination once, fuelled, and you will know your true payload for the life of the equipment.

Then when a broker says 45,000 lb of product, you know instantly whether it fits — rather than finding out at the scale after you have committed the day to it.

When you need a permit

Overweight or oversize permits are issued by each state, for a specific route, for a specific period. Practical points:

  • Get them before you move, not after being stopped. A permit does not retro-fix a violation.
  • They are route-specific. Deviating from the permitted route voids it.
  • Each state is separate. A four-state run needs four permits.
  • Price the permit and the extra time into the load before accepting. Permitted loads pay more for a reason, and the reason is that they cost more to run.
  • A permit does not override equipment ratings. Tyres, axles and the bridge posting still bind.

Price the scale stop and the permit into the load

Scale tickets, permits and the hours a weight problem costs are real costs. Put them in the fees field before you accept, not in your accounting afterwards.
