The real cost of deadhead miles

Empty miles do not appear on a rate confirmation. They appear in your bank account. Here is the arithmetic, and how to price a backhaul properly.

Deadhead does not appear on a rate confirmation. It appears in your bank account. It is the single most commonly underestimated cost in trucking, and the arithmetic showing why takes about four minutes.

What deadhead actually is

Deadhead is any mile the truck runs empty. Driving from where you delivered to where the next load picks up. Repositioning after a customer cancels. Running home at the end of a week because there was nothing going that direction.

Some people call it non-revenue miles, which is more honest: the truck is working, burning fuel and consuming hours of service, and nobody is paying for it.

Why it is the most misread number in freight

Two reasons, and they compound.

The first is that deadhead is invisible at the moment of decision. The rate confirmation says 850 miles. The broker says 850 miles. Nothing in the conversation mentions the 120 miles you will drive to reach the pickup, because from the broker's side those miles do not exist.

The second is that rate per mile — the industry's default shorthand — silently divides by the wrong number. When somebody says "that's $2.82 a mile", they have divided the rate by loaded miles. The truck ran 970.

Every mile costs the same whether the trailer is full or empty. The trailer does not know. The fuel gauge does not know. Only the invoice knows.

What an empty mile really costs

An empty mile costs everything a loaded mile costs, minus nothing:

ComponentPer mileRuns when empty?
Fuel ($3.85 ÷ 6.5 mpg)$0.592Yes — slightly less, since the trailer is lighter
Driver pay$0.620Yes — most carriers pay all miles
Maintenance and tyres$0.190Yes — wear is wear
Fixed cost of the truck$0.520Yes — the payment does not pause
Total per empty mile$1.922

Empty running is marginally more fuel-efficient because the load is lighter — perhaps five to eight per cent. That is the entire discount. Everything else is charged in full.

The number worth memorising

At a typical cost base, every empty mile costs roughly $1.90. A hundred miles of deadhead is about $190 gone before the load has earned a cent.

Work out your own figure once — it is your all-in cost per mile — and the arithmetic on any offer becomes something you can do in your head.

Look at the last two rows. By the number the industry quotes, these loads are identical at $2.82 a mile. By the number that pays your bills, one earns $604 and the other earns $86 — and Load B consumed an extra day of truck time to do it.

That $518 gap is not an unusual case. It is a 270-mile difference in deadhead, which is an ordinary Tuesday.

Why rate per mile lies about it

Rate per mile is not wrong as arithmetic. It is wrong as a denominator choice.

what everyone quotes: rate ÷ loaded_miles

what the truck runs: rate ÷ (loaded + deadhead)

The gap between those two figures is exactly the size of your deadhead problem on that load. In the example above it is 12 cents on Load A and 75 cents on Load B.

Call the second one your all-in rate per mile. It is the only version worth comparing between loads, and it is what the calculator on this site reports.

Pricing a backhaul properly

Here is where most carriers lose money without noticing: they evaluate the return leg on its own, as though refusing it were free.

It is not. If you refuse every return, you still drive home — empty, at $1.92 a mile.

The correct comparison is between three whole outcomes:

  1. Outbound, then deadhead home. The baseline. Whatever the outbound earned, minus the cost of the empty return.
  2. Outbound, then return load A. The whole round trip, both legs together.
  3. Outbound, then return load B. Same again.

Score the trip, never the leg.

When a cheap return beats a rich one

Return B pays $450 more and looks like the obvious choice. It is the worse choice by $165, because reaching it costs 180 extra empty miles and the longer route adds 140 loaded miles of cost the extra rate does not cover.

A cheap return that eliminates 300 empty miles beats a rich return that adds 200. The rate is not the variable that decides it.

Note also that both returns beat driving home empty by roughly $1,500. Even Return A, at a barely-break-even $16 on its own leg, is worth taking — because the alternative is not zero, it is minus $1,634.

Five ways to cut it

  1. Price the round trip before you accept the outbound. A good rate into a dead market is not a good rate. If you know the region has no outbound freight, the deadhead home belongs in the outbound decision.
  2. Track deadhead as a percentage, per lane. Lanes have persistent patterns. A lane that averages 22% deadhead is not unlucky; it is structurally expensive, and it should be priced that way or dropped.
  3. Trade rate for position. Taking $150 less to deliver somewhere with freight is profitable if it saves 200 empty miles — that is $380 of cost avoided for $150 given up.
  4. Widen the pickup radius rather than the rate. A load 40 miles further out at the same rate is usually better than one 40 miles closer at 10 cents less.
  5. Measure it honestly. Most carriers do not know their deadhead percentage. You cannot manage a number you have never calculated.

What percentage is normal

Deadhead is usually expressed as empty miles ÷ total miles:

RangeReading
Under 8%Very good. Usually dedicated freight or a dense operating area.
8–12%Normal for a well-run truckload operation.
12–18%Worth attention. Often one or two lanes doing the damage.
Over 18%Something structural. Usually a region with no outbound freight, or a habit of chasing rate over position.

These are working rules of thumb from operating experience, not a published statistic — treat them as a place to start rather than a benchmark to hit. Specialised equipment legitimately runs higher, and your own trend over time tells you more than any industry figure.

The number that matters is your own, measured monthly. The Lanes report in the console shows deadhead percentage per lane, which is where the fixable cases hide.
