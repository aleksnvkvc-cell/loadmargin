Tires, in cents per mile

Tires are a bigger, more controllable cost than most owner-operators realise. Measured right, in cents per mile, not per tire, the cheap tire is often the expensive one. Here is how to manage the second-largest maintenance line on the truck.

After the engine and its aftertreatment, tires are one of the biggest maintenance costs on a truck, and one of the most controllable. Owner-operators who buy the cheapest casing and ignore pressure quietly spend more per mile than those who buy well and check often. The trick is to measure tires the way you measure everything else: per mile.

Think in cents per mile, not per tire

A tire's price tells you almost nothing; its cost per mile tells you everything. A tire that costs more but lasts proportionally longer, or improves fuel economy, can be cheaper per mile than a bargain casing that wears fast and drags your mpg. Work it out:

tire cost per mile = tire price ÷ miles it lasts

Then add the fuel effect: a low-rolling-resistance tire that gains you a fraction of an mpg is worth real money over its life, often more than the price difference. See the fuel guide for how much an mpg is worth.

Why cheap tires are often expensive

The cheapest tire on the rack and the cheapest tire per mile are rarely the same tire. Buying on sticker is how you overpay slowly.

A budget casing that wears out in far fewer miles, cannot be retreaded, and costs you mpg is a false economy. A quality casing bought right, run at correct pressure, and retreaded once or twice can be dramatically cheaper per mile. The upfront price is the most visible number and the least important.

Pressure: the free money

The single highest-return tire habit costs nothing: correct inflation pressure. Under-inflation wears tires faster, wrecks fuel economy, runs the tire hot, and causes blowouts: the roadside failure that also becomes a violation and a delay. Check pressure regularly with a real gauge, not a boot, and check it cold.

Alignment and rotation

A truck out of alignment eats tires unevenly and pulls your fuel economy down. Irregular wear is the tell: feathering, cupping, one-side wear. Alignment and sensible rotation spread wear and add miles across the set. Both are cheap next to a set of tires worn out early.

Retreads: sensible or false economy?

Retreading a good casing, putting new tread on a sound body, is standard practice on drive and trailer positions and is genuinely economical when done on quality casings. It is not for steer tires, where a failure is most dangerous. The economics only work if you buy casings worth retreading in the first place, which is another reason the cheapest tire is often the wrong one.

The legal and safety floor

  • Minimum tread depth is regulated: below it is a violation and a real hazard
  • No exposed cord, no sidewall damage, no mismatch beyond limits
  • Steer tires held to a higher standard, where you do not run retreads and replace early

A tire failure is a blowout, a possible accident, a violation and a delay. The floor is not where you want to operate; it is the line you stay well above.

A tire management routine

  1. Check pressure regularly, cold, with a gauge. This alone pays for itself.
  2. Watch for irregular wear: it means alignment or pressure, both fixable.
  3. Buy on cost per mile, casing quality and fuel economy, not sticker.
  4. Retread quality casings on drives and trailers; buy new on steers.
  5. Track tire cost per mile in your books like any other line.

Tires belong in your number

Tires are a real, controllable cents-per-mile cost. Track them, and buy on cost per mile, not price.
