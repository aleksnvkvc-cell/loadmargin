Driver pay, and who carries the bad day

The same 2,450-mile week pays between $1,519 and $1,994 depending only on the model. That is 19 cents a mile of cost difference, and most carriers never measure it.

Driver pay is the largest controllable line in a carrier's cost structure and the one most often set by copying whatever the last employer did. The model matters as much as the rate, because the model decides who carries which risk.

The four models in use

ModelDriver is paid forWho carries the risk of a bad day
Cents per mileMiles turnedThe driver
Percentage of line haulA share of the revenueShared
HourlyTime on dutyThe carrier
Salary / guaranteedThe weekThe carrier

That third column is the whole subject. A dock that detains the truck four hours costs a cents-per-mile driver four hours of unpaid life and costs the carrier nothing extra. Under hourly, the same four hours cost the carrier directly. Neither is more moral; they produce very different behaviour.

Whatever you do not pay for, you will get less care about. Pay only for miles and you should not be surprised when nobody fights the receiver over dwell time.

Cents per mile

The default in US truckload. Typical range for a company driver runs roughly $0.50 to $0.75 per mile, with experience, region and equipment moving it.

Design decisionOptionsWhat it changes
Which milesPractical / HHG / hubPractical miles run 3–7% above household-goods mileage. Same rate, different pay
DeadheadPaid / unpaid / reducedUnpaid deadhead pushes dispatch problems onto the driver
Accessorial payStop, detention, layoverWithout these the model has a real gap

The mileage source is not a detail. Paying $0.62 on household-goods miles when the truck runs practical miles is a quiet 3–7% pay cut, and drivers with a phone work it out. Say which basis you use, in writing, in the pay agreement.

Strengths: pay tracks revenue closely, easy to forecast, easy to compare between employers. Weaknesses: pays nothing for the hours that are not driving, which is exactly where a bad week is lost, and creates pressure to keep moving.

Percentage of line haul

The driver takes a share of what the load billed — commonly 25–30% of line haul for a company driver on a carrier-owned truck, and 65–75% for an owner-operator leased on, where the percentage also has to cover their truck.

The three questions that define a percentage plan

Percentage of what? Line haul only, or line haul plus fuel surcharge? Including the surcharge hands the driver a share of a fuel reimbursement — usually wrong, always contentious if unstated.

Are accessorials included? Detention and stop pay are earned by the driver's time. Excluding them from the percentage while keeping them yourself is the fastest way to lose a good driver.

Does the driver see the rate confirmation? A percentage plan without rate transparency is a trust problem waiting to happen. Most carriers who run this model well simply show the rate.

Strengths: aligns incentives — the driver benefits from a well-priced load and from accessorials being collected. Self-adjusting in a rising or falling market. Weaknesses: driver income swings with the market, and it exposes your pricing.

Hourly

Uncommon in over-the-road, standard in local, drayage and dedicated work. Typical $22–$30 per hour with overtime rules depending on the exemption that applies.

Hourly is the honest model for work with unpredictable dwell — port drayage, grocery distribution, anything with a queue. It moves the cost of waiting onto the party who can actually negotiate about it, which is you, and that is a feature: a carrier paying hourly has a direct financial reason to bill detention properly.

Strengths: covers all working time, easy to explain, reduces pressure to drive tired. Weaknesses: pay is decoupled from revenue, so a slow dock costs you twice, and it requires accurate time records.

Salary and guaranteed pay

A fixed weekly figure, sometimes as a floor underneath a mileage plan ("guaranteed $1,250 or $0.62/mile, whichever is greater"). The guarantee variant is increasingly common because it solves recruitment without abandoning mileage economics.

Strengths: a recruiting advantage, stable driver income, simple payroll. Weaknesses: your largest variable cost becomes fixed, which is dangerous in a soft market — a guaranteed week on a truck that ran 1,400 miles is expensive.

The same week under four models

One driver, one week: 2,450 miles, 6 stops, 3 hours of detention, 58 hours on duty, $6,900 of line haul billed plus $1,050 of fuel surcharge and $220 of accessorials.

The spread from cheapest to dearest is $474.60 on one week — about $24,700 a year per driver. And notice the models are much closer together than the arguments about them suggest: five of the six land between 22% and 25% of line haul. The model mostly decides which weeks are good for the driver, not the long-run average.

Convert everything to one comparable number

Whatever model you run, compute driver cost per total mile every month. It is the only figure that plugs into a load calculation, and it is how you find out that your "$0.62 a mile" plan actually costs $0.71 once accessorial pay, unpaid deadhead policy and guarantees are counted.

The costs that are not the pay rate

Loaded cost per driver is well above the pay rate, and budgets that ignore this under-price every load:

ItemTypical annual

As a working rule, fully loaded driver cost is 1.25× to 1.4× the wage. A $0.62 per mile plan is a $0.78–$0.87 per mile cost, and that is the figure your load calculator needs.

The number that dwarfs all of it

Every argument about two cents per mile is smaller than the cost of the driver leaving.

Replacing a driver costs recruiting spend, orientation time, a truck sitting empty for one to three weeks, and a productivity ramp. Small-carrier estimates commonly land between $8,000 and $12,000 per replacement, and the idle-truck component alone — $195 a day of fixed cost at the illustrative base used on this site — is $1,400 a week.

Do the arithmetic before you refuse a raise

Two cents per mile on 120,000 miles a year is $2,400.

One replacement is $8,000 to $12,000, plus the two weeks the truck did not move.

If the two cents keeps a competent driver for a year, it pays for itself three to five times over. If it does not, it was not the two cents.

This is not an argument for paying more indiscriminately. It is an argument for treating pay as an investment with a measurable return, and for tracking turnover cost as a line item so the comparison can be made at all.

Choosing a model

  • Long-haul, predictable docks, experienced drivers — cents per mile with genuine accessorial pay. Simple, comparable, and the accessorial pay closes the gap that makes pure CPM unfair.
  • Unpredictable dwell — drayage, grocery, dedicated — hourly. Anything else transfers a cost the driver cannot control onto the driver.
  • Small fleet, high trust, sophisticated drivers — percentage. It aligns everyone with rate quality and accessorial collection, and it requires you to be comfortable showing the rate.
  • Recruiting into a tight market — a guarantee floor over a mileage plan. You keep the economics and remove the driver's downside, which is the thing they are actually worried about.

Whichever you choose, write it down completely — mileage basis, deadhead treatment, accessorial schedule, pay period, and what happens in a breakdown week. Most pay disputes are not about the rate. They are about a case nobody wrote down.
