IFTA, without the folklore

One licence, one quarterly return, three formulas. And one audit failure mode that turns a $150 bill into a four-figure one.

IFTA is not a tax. It is a settlement system for a tax you have already paid at the pump, redistributed to the states you actually drove through. Understanding that one sentence makes the whole filing obvious.

Why IFTA exists

Fuel tax is charged by the state, and the state charges it where you buy. A truck fuels in one jurisdiction and burns most of it in three others. Before 1996 that meant a separate permit and a separate return for every state a carrier touched.

The International Fuel Tax Agreement replaced that with one licence, one set of decals and one quarterly return, filed with your base jurisdiction. It covers the 48 contiguous US states and 10 Canadian provinces. Your base jurisdiction collects what you owe, or refunds what you overpaid, and settles with everyone else on your behalf.

Fuel tax follows the miles, not the pump. IFTA is the machinery that moves the money from where you bought the diesel to where you burned it.

Who has to register

You need an IFTA licence if you operate a qualified motor vehicle in two or more member jurisdictions. A qualified motor vehicle is one used to transport persons or property that meets any of:

  • Two axles and a gross vehicle weight over 26,000 lb; or
  • Three or more axles, regardless of weight; or
  • A combination weight over 26,000 lb.

Recreational vehicles are exempt. A truck that never leaves its home state does not need IFTA — it pays fuel tax at the pump and that is the end of it.

The four dates

The return is due on the last day of the month following the quarter. There are no exceptions worth planning around, and a weekend or holiday only ever moves the deadline forward to the next business day in some jurisdictions — never rely on it.

File even when you owe nothing

A zero return is still a return. A quarter with no interstate movement still has to be filed, and a quarter where you cannot pay still has to be filed — the late-filing penalty and the interest on unpaid tax are separate things, and filing on time stops the first one.

The calculation, step by step

Three steps. The whole quarter reduces to this.

1. fleet_mpg = total_miles ÷ total_gallons_purchased

2. taxable_gal[s] = miles_in_state[s] ÷ fleet_mpg

3. net_tax[s] = (taxable_gal[s] − tax_paid_gal[s]) × rate[s]

Note what step 1 does: it computes one fleet-wide mpg for the whole quarter and applies it to every state. IFTA does not care that you got 7.1 mpg across Kansas and 5.4 mpg over the Grapevine. It divides your total miles by your total gallons and uses that everywhere.

Step 3 is where the settlement happens. taxable_gal is the fuel the state says you burned there. tax_paid_gal is the fuel you actually bought there, on which you already paid that state's tax at the pump. The difference, times the state's rate, is what you owe — or, if negative, a credit.

The one lever you control

Total taxable gallons always equal total gallons purchased — the system is closed. So IFTA is not a cost you can reduce by buying less fuel. It is a cost you shift by choosing where to buy: pump price plus that state's fuel tax, compared across your route, is the real decision. A cheap pump price in a high-tax state can still be the right buy, and a cheap pump price in a state you barely drive through is a credit you have to wait three months to see.

Taxable miles versus total miles

These are not the same number, and mixing them up is the most common filing error.

Total miles are every mile the qualified vehicle turned in the quarter, in every jurisdiction, including non-IFTA ones.

Taxable miles are total miles less anything the jurisdiction exempts. Exemptions vary by state and commonly include specific toll roads, off-highway or private-property miles, and certain fuel-trip-permit miles. There is no universal list — the exemptions are set by each jurisdiction, and a mile exempt in one state is taxable in the next.

Fleet mpg in step 1 is computed on total miles. Step 2 distributes on taxable miles per state. Getting that backwards changes every line of the return.

What you must keep

The filing is the easy part; the audit is not. IFTA record retention is four years from the return due date or filing date, whichever is later, and the records have to support every number on the return.

  • Distance records for every trip: date, origin, destination, route, beginning and ending odometer, and miles by jurisdiction. GPS or ELD data satisfies this if it is retained and readable.
  • Fuel receipts for every purchase claimed as tax-paid: date, seller name and address, gallons, price per gallon, unit number and purchaser name. A receipt missing the unit number is a receipt an auditor can strike.
  • Bulk fuel records if you have your own tank: withdrawals by vehicle, plus inventory reconciliation.

The audit failure mode

An auditor who cannot verify your mileage does not throw out the return — they recompute it at a standard mpg, typically 4.0. At 4.0 mpg the same 24,800 miles generate 6,200 taxable gallons instead of 4,000. You are then billed the tax on 2,200 gallons you never burned, plus penalty and interest. That is the real cost of a shoebox of receipts.

What it costs to get it wrong

The late-filing penalty is $50 or 10% of the tax due, whichever is greater, and it applies to the return, not to the amount. A return filed a day late on a quarter where you owed nothing still attracts the $50.

Interest accrues separately on unpaid tax, per jurisdiction, per month. Repeated late filing leads to licence revocation, and a revoked IFTA licence means the decals come off and the truck is limited to its base state or to trip permits bought at the border — which is a fine, a delay and a fee at every crossing.

What IFTA does to your cost per mile

Most carriers never put IFTA in their cost model at all, because the quarterly settlement is small and lumpy. That is a mistake of accounting, not of size: the fuel tax was always part of the cost of a mile, it was simply paid at the pump.

The clean way to handle it: your fuel cost per mile should be the pump price including tax — which is what the DOE index reports and what your receipts show — and the quarterly IFTA settlement is then a small true-up, positive or negative, that belongs in overhead rather than in the per-mile figure.

Double-counting is the risk. If you model diesel at a tax-inclusive price and add an IFTA line to cost per mile, you have charged the same tax twice and every load looks worse than it is.
