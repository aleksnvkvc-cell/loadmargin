ELD rules, and the exemptions people get wrong

An exemption is not a category you belong to — it is a test you pass or fail each day. Here is each one, what it actually requires, and the records you keep either way.

Most drivers who think they are exempt are not, and a fair number who are running an ELD did not have to. The exemptions are narrow, they are written as tests you either pass every single day or you do not, and losing one mid-day means you were supposed to be on a log.

Who actually has to run an ELD

The starting rule is simple: if you are required to keep a record of duty status, you keep it on an ELD. Everything below is an exception to that, and each one is conditional.

The exceptions do not travel with the driver or the truck as a permanent status. They apply per day. A short-haul driver who goes 160 air miles on a Tuesday needed a log for Tuesday, and the fact that Monday was compliant does not help.

An exemption is not a category you belong to. It is a test you pass or fail each day, and you find out which at the end of the day.

The short-haul exception

49 CFR § 395.1(e)(1) — the one most local and regional CDL drivers use. All of these must hold:

ConditionLimit
Operate within a radius of the normal work reporting location150 air miles
Return to that same reporting location and be releasedwithin 14 hours
Driving time11 hours maximum
Start and end at the same reporting locationevery shift
Employer keeps time records showing start, end and total hours6 months

Air miles, not road miles. 150 air miles is a straight-line radius — roughly 172 statute miles. The driving distance to get there is irrelevant; what matters is how far the furthest point is from the yard as the crow flies.

The 14 hours is the one that breaks

The radius is usually predictable. The 14-hour return is not — one long detention at a receiver and a driver who left the yard at 06:00 gets released at 20:40, and that day needed a record of duty status that nobody was creating.

This is why carriers running close to the edge put an ELD in the truck anyway and simply use the short-haul setting: the device records the day either way, and the exception days cost nothing.

The non-CDL 100 air-mile version

§ 395.1(e)(2) covers drivers operating vehicles that do not require a CDL. It is tighter on time and distance but looser in one respect:

  • 100 air-mile radius of the work reporting location
  • Released within 14 hours on any five days of the week, and within 16 hours on up to two days
  • 11 hours driving maximum
  • Same time records kept by the employer

Hotshot operators running under 26,001 lb combined frequently sit here, and frequently do not realise it, because the CDL short-haul rule is the one everyone quotes.

Eight days in thirty

A driver who keeps a record of duty status on eight days or fewer in any rolling 30-day period may use paper logs on those days instead of an ELD.

This is the exception that fits the genuinely occasional driver — the mechanic who moves a truck twice a month, the owner who covers a run when someone is sick, the seasonal operation.

Rolling, not calendar

It is any 30 consecutive days, counted backwards from today, not a calendar month. Nine logged days inside any such window and day nine needed an ELD.

Count them deliberately. Somebody has to be keeping the tally, and "it felt like about a week" is not a defence at an audit.

Pre-2000 engines: the model year, not the truck

A vehicle with an engine model year of 1999 or older is not required to run an ELD. This is the exemption most misunderstood in the industry, so read the next line twice.

It is the engine's model year, from the engine plate — not the truck's model year, not the year it was built, not the year it was registered.

A 2003 glider with a rebuilt 1998 engine qualifies. A 1999 truck that had a 2005 engine dropped in it does not. Enforcement checks the engine data plate, so:

  • Photograph the engine plate and keep the picture in the cab
  • Keep the rebuild or repower paperwork with the registration
  • Expect to be asked, because this exemption is claimed far more often than it is true

You still have to keep a record of duty status. Paper logs, kept properly, for the whole day.

Driveaway-towaway and agricultural

Driveaway-towaway — § 395.1(e)(3) — where the vehicle being driven is the commodity being delivered, or is part of the shipment. Truck delivery drivers, RV transporters.

Agricultural — § 395.1(k) — hours-of-service rules are suspended entirely for the transport of agricultural commodities within a 150 air-mile radius of the source during planting and harvesting season, as each state defines that season. No HOS means no log means no ELD, inside that radius. Cross out of it and the clock starts from the point you left the radius.

What you still have to keep when you are exempt

Exempt from the device is not exempt from the record. Auditors ask for these, and their absence is what turns a clean operation into a finding:

  • Time records — start time, release time, total hours on duty, for every short-haul day, kept six months
  • Paper RODS for the eight-days-in-thirty days, and for pre-2000 engine vehicles, kept six months
  • Supporting documents — bills of lading, fuel receipts, dispatch records, payroll — kept six months and matching the hours claimed
  • Evidence of the exemption itself: the engine plate photograph, the radius your reporting location actually implies

The hours-of-service limits themselves — 11 driving, 14 on-duty window, 30-minute break, 60/70 hour cycles — apply exactly the same whether or not you are on a device. See the hours of service guide for how the clocks interact.

Choosing a device without getting stuck

ELDs are self-certified by the vendor and listed on the FMCSA registry. FMCSA has removed devices from that registry before, and when that happens every carrier running them has a short window to replace them. So:

  1. Check the device is currently on the FMCSA registered list — and check again at renewal, not just at purchase.
  2. Ask what happens to your data if you leave. Six months of records are a legal obligation. Export format matters.
  3. Read the contract term. Three-year lock-ins on hardware that may be delisted is the specific risk here.
  4. Confirm the transfer methods it supports — web services, email, USB, Bluetooth. An inspector will pick one.
  5. Cost per truck per month, all in, including the hardware, the cellular data and any per-report fees.

At a roadside stop

Three things must be in the vehicle. Missing any of them is a violation on its own, independent of your actual hours:

  1. The ELD user manual
  2. An instruction sheet for transferring data to an inspector
  3. A supply of blank paper RODS — at least eight days' worth

Keep them together, physically, in one envelope. The inspection is a poor time to discover the manual is on a laptop at home.

Edits, unassigned time and the audit trail

An ELD records driving time automatically, and automatically recorded driving time can never be edited — not by the driver, not by the carrier, not by the vendor. Everything else can be annotated, but every edit is retained alongside the original with a reason.

Two patterns that draw attention in an audit

Unassigned driving time. Every mile the truck moved has to be accounted for. Large blocks of unassigned time suggest somebody drove without logging in, and it is one of the first things an investigator looks for. Assign it or annotate it — yard moves, mechanics, shunting — promptly, not in a batch at month end.

Carrier edits that reduce driving time. The carrier may propose an edit; the driver must accept or reject it, and the original stays. A pattern of proposed edits shaving driving hours is the fastest way to turn a records review into something worse.

When the device fails

Malfunctions happen and the rule anticipates them. What it requires:

  1. Note the malfunction and inform the carrier within 24 hours, in writing
  2. Reconstruct the current 24 hours and the previous seven days on paper, unless the device can still produce them
  3. Continue on paper RODS until the device is working
  4. The carrier has eight days to repair or replace it, and may request an extension from the FMCSA field office beyond that

This is exactly why blank paper logs are a required item in the cab. A driver with a dead device and no blank logs has two violations instead of none.

What it costs, and where it pays for itself

ItemTypical range
Hardware, per truck$0–$500
Subscription, per truck per month$15–$45
Annual, one truck$180–$540

Ranges vary widely by vendor and by what is bundled — IFTA mileage, maintenance reminders, GPS tracking are often the same subscription. If it is producing your IFTA mileage by jurisdiction, it has already replaced several hours of quarterly work.

The genuine argument for a device on an exempt operation is different from compliance: it settles detention arguments. A timestamped record of arrival and departure is what turns a detention claim from a conversation into an invoice.

Put the compliance cost in the fixed number

Subscriptions, permits and filings are fixed overhead — they run whether the truck moves or not, and they belong in your cost per mile.
