Retirement, the plan nobody sets up for you now

A company match quietly built a company driver's retirement. Independent, that is entirely on you — and the truck is not it. Here are the tax-advantaged accounts a self-employed driver can use, and why starting small beats starting never.

A company driver's retirement was partly built by a matched 401(k) they barely thought about. Go independent and that stops — nobody sets up a retirement plan for a self-employed driver, and nobody matches your contributions. If you do not build it, it does not get built. And the common fallback plan — “the truck is my retirement” — is a trap.

Why this is on you now

Independence moves every benefit onto you: health insurance, paid time off, and retirement. The upside of being your own boss is real, but so is the responsibility. Retirement is the one that is easiest to postpone forever, because nothing forces it — which is exactly why so many owner-operators reach the end of a long career with a worn-out truck and little else.

Nobody is going to make you save. That freedom is the whole risk — a plan that is never automatic is a plan that never happens unless you make it happen.

The truck is not a retirement plan

A depreciating asset that needs constant money to keep running is not a nest egg. The truck will be worth a fraction of its price when you stop, and it earns nothing once you park it. Selling the last truck funds a few months, not a retirement. The business is your income; retirement has to be built separately, out of the profit, into accounts that grow whether or not you are driving.

The SEP-IRA

Simple, high limits, popular with the self-employed

A SEP-IRA lets a self-employed person contribute a percentage of net earnings up to a generous annual cap — far more than a regular IRA — with the contribution generally tax-deductible. It is simple to open and flexible: contribute more in a good year, less in a lean one. For many owner-operators it is the default choice. The exact percentage and dollar limits are set by the IRS and change, so confirm the current figures with an accountant.

The Solo 401(k)

A Solo (individual) 401(k) is for a business with no employees other than you (and a spouse). It can allow larger total contributions than a SEP at some income levels because you contribute both as “employee” and “employer,” and some versions allow Roth contributions and loans. It is a little more paperwork than a SEP but can shelter more — another accountant conversation, because the best choice depends on your numbers.

Plain IRAs

A traditional or Roth IRA has much lower contribution limits but is dead simple and available to almost anyone with earned income. A Roth grows tax-free, which is powerful over a long career. An IRA is a fine place to start — and starting is the whole point — even if you later move up to a SEP or Solo 401(k) as profit grows.

Starting small beats starting never

  1. Open something now — even a small automatic monthly contribution. Time in the market does more than the amount early on.
  2. Automate it so it happens without a decision each month, like a bill.
  3. Raise it in good years — bank part of the peak season into it.
  4. Ask an accountant which account fits your income and how the deduction helps.

The single biggest mistake is waiting until the business “can afford it,” which somehow never arrives. A small amount, automatic, starting now, beats a big plan that starts someday.

Retirement is a business expense to yourself

Pay your future self like a bill, out of the profit, into an account that grows without the truck. Build it into how you run, not into what is left over.
