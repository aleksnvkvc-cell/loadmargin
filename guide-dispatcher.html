A dispatcher, or do it yourself?

A dispatcher takes 5–10% to find your loads and handle the phone. Whether that is a bargain or a leak depends entirely on whether they negotiate better than you would — here is how to judge it, in money.

A dispatcher finds your loads, negotiates, handles the broker calls and the paperwork, and takes a percentage of the revenue. Whether that percentage is money well spent or a quiet leak comes down to one question: do they get you better loads than you would get yourself? Often yes. Sometimes very much no.

What a dispatcher actually does

A dispatcher is a back-office service, not a boss. Typically they:

  • Find loads on the boards and through their broker contacts
  • Negotiate the rate
  • Handle the broker setup and rate cons
  • Sort out problems — a missed appointment, a detention claim
  • Sometimes handle paperwork and invoicing

They work for you. You own the authority, the truck and the risk; they are hired help.

What the percentage costs in cents per mile

The fee is usually 5–10% of gross, sometimes a flat weekly amount. Put it in cents per mile so you can see it against everything else:

Gross at $2.10/mile, 8% dispatch feeabout 17¢/mile
Same, 10%about 21¢/mile
Flat $500/week over 2,500 miles20¢/mile

Seventeen to twenty-one cents a mile is a large number — comparable to your maintenance or a chunk of your fixed cost. It is only worth it if the dispatcher adds more than that in better rates and saved time.

The honest case for a dispatcher

  • You cannot drive and work the phones at once. Hours spent booking are hours not driving; a dispatcher keeps the truck moving.
  • A good one negotiates better than a tired driver at 8pm, and has broker relationships you do not.
  • They absorb the admin — setups, rate cons, problem calls.
  • For a new carrier, they shortcut the learning curve on rates and lanes.

The honest case against

  • The percentage is real money, every load, forever.
  • You do not learn your own market — dependency is a risk if the dispatcher leaves or underperforms.
  • Their incentive is volume, not your margin — a dispatcher paid on gross is paid to keep you moving, even on a load that barely pays.

The dispatcher who costs you money

The tell: books fast and cheap

A weak dispatcher earns their percentage by booking quickly, not by booking well — taking the first board load at the posted rate so the truck moves and their fee is earned. That is a service you are paying 8% for that you could do yourself, badly, for free.

The test is simple: are the loads they book better than what you see on the board yourself, after their cut? If a dispatcher takes 8% and books the same loads you would have, they are a 17¢ per mile leak. If they consistently beat the board by more than their fee, they are worth it.

The compliance line a dispatcher must not cross

A dispatcher works under your authority as your agent. They are not a broker and must not act like one — re-brokering your freight, or brokering to other carriers, without proper broker authority is a real problem. Make sure the arrangement is a genuine dispatch service for your authority, not someone using you to move freight they should be brokering with a licence and a bond.

How to decide, in numbers

  1. Work out the dispatch fee in cents per mile at your typical rate.
  2. Ask whether their loads beat the public board by more than that, after the fee.
  3. Value your own time — driving hours are worth more than booking hours.
  4. Start with a trial and a clear exit; compare a month with them against a month of self-dispatch on your own numbers.

Self-dispatching keeps the whole rate and teaches you your market, at the cost of your time and a steeper learning curve. A genuinely good dispatcher is worth their cut. A mediocre one is a tax on every mile. Judge them in cents per mile, not on the convenience.

Put the dispatch fee in your cost per mile

5–10% of gross is 17–21¢ a mile of real cost. It belongs in your number, and it makes the “do they earn it” question answerable.
