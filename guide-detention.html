Detention, and the hour you cannot bill for

A detained hour costs about $156. The market pays $40 to $75 for it. Here is the whole calculation, and how to price the wait before you accept the load.

Detention pay is not compensation. At the rates the market pays, it is a partial refund on a loss you have already taken — and the part it never refunds is the hours of service the wait consumed.

What detention actually costs

A truck sitting at a dock is losing money in three separate ways at once, and only one of them shows up on the invoice.

  1. Fixed cost keeps running. The truck payment, the insurance, the permits and the overhead accrue per day regardless of whether the wheels turn.
  2. Hours of service are consumed. The 14-hour on-duty window does not pause for a slow receiver. Every hour at the dock is an hour permanently removed from that day's driving capacity.
  3. The next load moves out of reach. A three-hour wait does not delay you three hours. It frequently delays you a full day, because the delivery appointment you could have made is now tomorrow's.
Detention pay compensates you for the first of those three. The second and third are usually larger, and nobody bills for them.

The clock you cannot bill for

This is the part that makes detention structurally worse than it looks.

The 14-hour window starts when the driver goes on duty and runs continuously until it expires. It does not stop for loading, for a dock queue, for lumper paperwork, or for anything except the qualifying sleeper-berth splits. Eleven of those fourteen hours may be spent driving; the other three are the entire budget for fuelling, inspections, scaling and every dock in the day.

The real cost of a detained hour

Work it from the truck's daily economics rather than from a per-hour rate, because the per-hour rate is the thing being disputed.

The direct cost is $39. That is the number a broker will argue about. The number that matters to your year is $156, and it is the one that never appears in a detention negotiation because the opportunity cost is invisible from the other side of the phone.

Which number to use, and when

Use the direct cost when you are deciding whether a specific wait was worth absorbing on a load you already committed to — the fixed cost is sunk either way.

Use the full economic cost when you are deciding whether to take a load from a facility with known dwell problems, or when setting a policy. That decision is made before the day is committed, so the lost capacity is real.

What detention typically pays

Common market terms, which are conventions rather than rules — every one of these is negotiable and every one of them is written into the rate confirmation or it does not exist:

Notice that the top of the detention range, $75 an hour, is still less than half the full economic cost of $156. Detention is not a profit centre and no clause will make it one. Its purpose is to make the shipper's dwell time expensive enough to fix.

Why detention is billed and not collected

Carriers routinely bill detention and routinely do not get paid for it. The reasons are almost always procedural, and all four are fixable before the truck ever arrives.

  1. No time stamps the shipper agreed to. A driver's word is not evidence. In-and out times must be signed on the bill of lading, or captured by a gate system, or documented by geofenced ELD data with the arrival and departure recorded.
  2. The clause was never in the confirmation. If detention terms are not written on the rate confirmation before dispatch, you are asking for a favour afterwards.
  3. It was billed late. Many contracts require accessorial claims within a fixed window — often 7 to 30 days. Bill it with the original invoice, not as an afterthought.
  4. The driver did not notify anyone in real time. Most terms require notice at the point free time expires. A call logged at hour 2 is worth several hundred dollars; the same call at hour 5 is worth nothing.

Four fields, most of the money

Arrival time, departure time, a signature, and the notification timestamp. If those four exist for every stop, detention collection stops being a negotiation and becomes an invoice. If any one is missing, it is a negotiation you will usually lose.

Two stop-offs, one driver assist and a tarp on a single load is $300 to $700. On a fleet running forty loads a week, systematically not billing half of them is a six-figure annual number.

Scoring a load with detention risk

The right way to handle a facility with a bad reputation is not to refuse it. It is to price the expected wait into the load before accepting.

expected_detention_cost = p(delay) × hours × full_hourly_cost

adjusted_net = normal_net − expected_detention_cost + expected_detention_pay

A facility that detains you three hours on 60% of visits, at $156 an hour full cost and $50 an hour paid after two hours free:

Expected cost0.60 × 3 × $156 = −$280.80
Expected pay0.60 × 1 × $50 = +$30.00
Net adjustment to the load−$250.80

That load needs $251 more than an identical load from a facility that loads in an hour. Not a refusal — a price. The calculator on this site has a detention and idle field for exactly this: put your expected wait in before you accept, not your actual wait afterwards.

A detention policy that works

  1. Terms on every confirmation. Free time, hourly rate, increment, cap, TONU and layover — before dispatch, in writing, no exceptions for a good broker.
  2. Driver notifies at free-time expiry. Set it as a hard rule with a timestamped message, not a phone call nobody logged.
  3. Signed in and out times on every BOL. If the facility will not sign, the driver photographs the gate log or the dock clock with the phone's timestamp on.
  4. Bill accessorials with the original invoice. Never separately, never later.
  5. Track dwell by facility, not by broker. The broker did not make you wait; the receiver did. After twenty visits you will know which four facilities cost you a day a month, and those are the ones to reprice or drop.

That last point is the one with compounding value. A carrier who can say "your Columbus DC averages 3.4 hours against a 2-hour free window across our last eleven visits, here are the timestamps" is not complaining. They are negotiating, and they usually win.
