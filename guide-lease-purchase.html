Lease-purchase, and the nine clauses that decide it

The gross is three times a company driver's. The take-home can be lower. Both facts live in the same contract, and the recruiting advert shows you only one.

A lease-purchase can be a legitimate route into truck ownership. It can also be a structure where you carry the costs of an owner and the freedom of an employee, and end the week owing money. The two look identical in the recruiting advert. They differ in about nine clauses.

What a lease-purchase actually is

A carrier leases you a truck. You drive for that carrier, they deduct the lease payment and other costs from your settlement, and at the end of the term you may own the truck — usually after a balloon payment.

The structural fact that matters: the same company supplies your truck and your freight. They set the lease payment and they decide which loads you get. If your income falls short, the party who could fix it by dispatching you better is the same party collecting your payment.

In a normal truck purchase, your lender and your customer are different people with different interests. In a lease-purchase they are the same people. That is not automatically predatory — but every problem in these arrangements comes from it.

Why it is appealing, honestly

It would be dishonest to present these as purely a trap. They solve real problems:

  • No down payment and no credit check. A driver who cannot get truck financing can start next week.
  • No authority to obtain. No insurance to buy, no MC number, no bond, no IFTA registration — see what that would otherwise cost in the authority guide.
  • Freight is provided. No sales, no load boards, no collections.
  • A path to ownership for someone who genuinely has no other one.

For a driver with no capital and no credit, those are not small things. The question is not whether the appeal is real — it is whether the specific contract in front of you converts that appeal into ownership or into a treadmill.

The number that decides it

Ask for the last twelve weeks of settlement statements from three drivers currently in the programme — not projections, not averages, actual statements with the deductions visible.

A carrier running an honest programme can produce these. One that will not, or that offers a "typical example" instead, has told you the answer.

The phrase to distrust

Nearly every programme advertises a walk-away lease. OOIDA, which reviews these contracts constantly, puts it bluntly: "All these carriers say walk-away lease, walk-away lease. I've still never seen a walk-away lease."

What the phrase usually means in the actual document: you may return the truck, and you remain liable for some combination of remaining payments, a return fee, damage assessments, the difference between the truck's value and the balance, and your escrow — which you do not get back. OOIDA reports that carriers do pursue drivers who leave, including against personal assets.

So: find the clause. Not the recruiter's description of it — the clause. If the contract does not contain a clean sentence saying you may return the truck and owe nothing further, it is not a walk-away lease, whatever the advert says.

Eleven clauses to read before you sign

  1. Termination. Exactly what you owe if you leave in month three. Get the number.
  2. Forced dispatch. Can you refuse a load? If not, you cannot price your own freight, which removes the main advantage of being an owner.
  3. Who chooses your loads, and on what basis. If lease drivers are dispatched after company drivers, your revenue is set by someone else's spare capacity.
  4. Maintenance escrow. Who holds it, who authorises spending, what happens to the balance when you leave, and whether it earns interest.
  5. Where repairs must be done. In-house-only clauses let the lessor set the price of your largest variable cost.
  6. The balloon payment. The amount at the end, and what the truck will plausibly be worth then. If the balloon exceeds the resale value, the "purchase" was never the point.
  7. Deduction ceiling. Is there any cap on weekly deductions? Without one, a bad week can settle at zero or negative.
  8. Negative settlement carry-over. If a week goes negative, does the shortfall roll into next week? This is how drivers end up owing money for months.
  9. Downtime. Does the lease payment continue while the truck is in the shop? It usually does. Ask what happens over a three-week engine repair.
  10. Insurance deductions. What cover you are actually being charged for, and whether you would be covered driving the truck off-dispatch.
  11. Title transfer. The precise conditions under which the truck becomes yours, in writing, with the mileage and the payment count.

Before you sign anything

Have someone independent read it. OOIDA reviews lease-purchase contracts for drivers and has seen more of them than anyone. A trucking attorney is the other option. Either costs a fraction of one bad month.

A carrier who objects to you having the contract reviewed has answered a question you did not have to ask.

The middle column that most people never consider is the fourth-from-left: buy a modest used truck and lease it onto a carrier. It needs a down payment, but you own the asset from day one, you can leave whenever you like, and the carrier still supplies freight. For a driver with some savings it is usually the better version of the same idea.

When it does work

These programmes are not uniformly bad, and drivers do finish them. The ones who do tend to share a few things:

  • They ran the arithmetic first and knew their weekly break-even before signing.
  • They had the contract reviewed by someone independent.
  • They chose a carrier with freight they could actually get miles on, not the highest advertised gross.
  • They kept a genuine reserve, so a breakdown week did not become a negative settlement.
  • They treated it as a business with a cost per mile — not as a better-paying driving job.

That last point is the whole difference. A lease driver who knows their cost per mile can tell whether this week worked. One who watches the gross cannot, and will not find out until the escrow is gone.

If you are already in one

  1. Work out your real cost per mile this week, including the lease payment and every deduction. Use the builder. You cannot decide anything without it.
  2. Read the termination clause properly and get the actual exit number.
  3. Compare three outcomes honestly: finishing the lease, exiting now and paying the cost, or going back to company driving. Sometimes finishing is right; sometimes the exit cost is less than another eight months of losses.
  4. Get advice before you walk. Leaving without understanding the liability is how a bad lease becomes a judgment against your house.
  5. Document everything — settlements, deduction statements, maintenance authorisations, dispatch records. If there is ever a dispute, that record is the case.
