Fuel surcharges, and the number nobody negotiates

A surcharge is not extra money. It is a mechanism for moving diesel price risk onto the shipper — and one unexamined input in it decides whether it works.

A fuel surcharge is not extra money. It is a mechanism for moving one specific risk — the price of diesel between the day a rate was agreed and the day the truck runs — off the carrier and onto the shipper. Treated as extra money, it quietly hides a loss.

What a fuel surcharge is

Line haul rates are negotiated in advance, sometimes months in advance on a contract lane. Diesel is not. It moves weekly, and it has moved by more than a dollar a gallon inside a single year more than once in the last decade.

Rather than reprice every lane every week, the industry splits the rate in two:

all-in rate = line haul + fuel surcharge

The line haul is meant to cover everything that does not move week to week — the truck, the driver, maintenance, insurance, overhead and margin. The surcharge is meant to cover the movement in diesel, and nothing else.

A surcharge is compensation for a risk you no longer carry. It is not profit, and a lane whose line haul only works because the surcharge is high is a lane that fails the moment diesel falls.

The DOE index it is pegged to

Nearly every surcharge in North American freight is pegged to one number: the US Energy Information Administration weekly retail on-highway diesel price, usually called the DOE or EIA index.

What it actually is, since a lot of money rides on a figure most people have never looked up:

  • It is the cash, self-serve price at the pump including taxes, for ultra-low sulfur diesel, captured at 8:00 a.m. local time on Monday.
  • It comes from a sample of 590 retail outlets drawn from a frame of roughly 73,000 service stations and 9,500 truck stops in the contiguous United States.
  • It is published around 10:00 a.m. Eastern on Tuesday, reporting Monday's prices — Wednesday when Tuesday is a federal holiday.
  • It is issued nationally, by PADD region, and separately for California. Alaska and Hawaii are outside the survey.

Two consequences follow that carriers routinely miss. First, the index is a retail pump price — if you buy on a fuel network at a discount, the index overstates what you pay, and that gap is yours to keep. Second, it is a week behind. In a rising market your surcharge is always chasing; in a falling market it is briefly generous.

The formula, in one line

Every surcharge scheme in use is a variation on the same arithmetic:

fsc_per_mile = (doe_price − base_price) ÷ peg_mpg

fsc_total = fsc_per_mile × miles

Three inputs, all negotiated, none of them handed down from anywhere:

InputTypicalWhat it means
DOE pricethis week's indexThe published national or regional average
Base price$1.20 – $1.50The diesel price the line haul rate already assumes
Peg mpg5.0 – 6.5The fuel economy the surcharge assumes your truck gets

A worked example at a $3.85 index, a $1.25 base and a 6.0 peg:

(3.85 − 1.25) ÷ 6.0 = $0.4333 per mile

850 miles × 0.4333 = $368.33 surcharge

Choosing a base price and a peg mpg

These two numbers decide whether the surcharge protects you or quietly transfers money away from you, and they are the part of the negotiation nobody argues about.

The base price is a claim about your line haul. Setting a base of $1.25 asserts that the line haul rate already covers diesel up to $1.25 a gallon. If the rate was actually built on $3.00 diesel, a $1.25 base means the shipper is paying a surcharge for fuel you were already being paid for — pleasant, but it will be discovered. If the rate was built on $3.85 diesel and the base is $1.25, you are being paid twice for the same gallon and the lane is priced above the market without either side realising why.

The peg mpg is where the money actually leaks. The peg divides. A higher peg means a smaller surcharge.

A shipper moving the peg from 6.0 to 7.0 has cut your surcharge by $52.62 on one load without touching the headline rate. Across two hundred loads a year that is $10,524. Nothing on the rate confirmation changed.

The only honest peg

The peg should be the mpg your fleet actually achieves, measured tank to tank, not the number on the OEM brochure. If your real figure is 6.2 and you agreed a peg of 7.0, the surcharge under-recovers on every single mile, permanently.

Reading a surcharge matrix

Many shippers do not use the formula directly. They publish a matrix: a table of diesel price bands against cents per mile, usually stepping in 5-cent or 10-cent bands of diesel.

DOE price bandFSC per mileImplied peg mpg
$3.50 – $3.599$0.386.05
$3.60 – $3.699$0.396.13
$3.70 – $3.799$0.416.02
$3.80 – $3.899$0.426.11
$3.90 – $3.999$0.446.03

The third column is the useful one and it is never printed. Take the midpoint of each band, take the stated base price, and divide: (midpoint − base) ÷ fsc_per_mile gives you the peg mpg the matrix is really using. If that number drifts upward as diesel rises, the matrix is regressive — it recovers proportionally less exactly when fuel hurts most.

Check the bands at the top of the table specifically. Some matrices simply stop at a ceiling, so above $5.00 diesel there is no additional recovery at all.

Where the surcharge stops covering fuel

Even a fair surcharge under-recovers, for a structural reason: the surcharge pays on loaded miles, and you burn fuel on all of them.

A load with 850 loaded miles and 120 miles of deadhead, at $3.85 diesel and a true 6.2 mpg:

Fuel actually burned(850 + 120) ÷ 6.2 × $3.85 = $602.34
Surcharge received850 × $0.4333 = $368.33
Uncovered fuel$234.01

Of that gap, roughly $74 is deadhead nobody is paying for and the rest is the base price — the part the line haul is supposed to carry. That is the design working as intended. The failure mode is forgetting it, seeing $368 of surcharge on the confirmation and treating fuel as handled.

The surcharge covers the movement in diesel above the base. It never covered all of your fuel, and it was never meant to.

Why you should always quote all-in

Two offers on the same lane:

Broker ABroker B
Line haul$2,400$2,150
Fuel surcharge$0$368
All-in$2,400$2,518
Rate per loaded mile quoted$2.82$2.53
All-in per loaded mile$2.82$2.96

Broker B's quoted rate per mile is 29 cents lower and Broker B is paying you $118 more. Carriers turn down loads over this every week.

The rule is simple and it removes an entire category of mistake: compare all-in, always, and confirm in writing whether a quoted rate includes fuel. The calculator on this site takes a single rate figure for exactly this reason — enter the all-in.

Four mistakes that cost real money

  1. Treating surcharge as margin. A month with high diesel shows high surcharge revenue and identical profit. If your reporting counts it as revenue growth, you will conclude the lane improved when nothing changed.
  2. Accepting the peg without checking your own mpg. The single most expensive unexamined number in a contract. Measure tank to tank over a quarter, then negotiate.
  3. Not knowing which index week applies. "The DOE" is not specific enough. The contract should say national or regional, and which week — Monday's publication for loads picking up that week is the common convention, but it must be written down.
  4. Forgetting the surcharge on accessorial-heavy runs. Multi-stop and layover loads burn fuel on repositioning that no surcharge scheme pays for. Score those on total miles, not on the stops.
