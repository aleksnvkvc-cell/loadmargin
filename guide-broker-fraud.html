Vetting brokers, and the fifteen minutes that pays for itself

One uncollectable invoice takes ten clean loads to replace. The defence is not suspicion — it is the same short routine, run on every new counterparty, every time.

Freight fraud is now an operating cost, not an edge case. The defence is not suspicion — it is a fifteen-minute routine that you run identically on every new counterparty, and a habit of writing things down.

The size of the problem

The Transportation Intermediaries Association estimates annual carrier losses to freight fraud in the range of $700 million to $1 billion. FMCSA complaint volumes have risen several-fold since 2021.

What makes it worse for a small carrier is the concentration: a fleet of four trucks running forty loads a month is exposed to one uncollectable $2,800 invoice as a full week of net profit at a 15% margin. A large carrier absorbs the same event as a rounding error.

You do not need to catch every scheme. You need your routine to be expensive enough to fail that whoever is running the scheme goes and finds an easier carrier.

The four things people mean by fraud

TypeWhat happensWho eats it
Double brokeringA party accepts a load as a carrier, then re-brokers it to you without authority. The original broker pays them; they disappearYou — you have no contract with whoever actually pays
Identity theftSomebody uses a real, authorised broker's or carrier's MC number, name and paperwork with their own phone and emailYou, and the impersonated company's reputation
Cargo theftThe "carrier" collecting the freight is fictitious; the load is stolen at pickupThe shipper, and whoever's insurance is nearest
Simple non-paymentA real, authorised broker that does not pay, slowly or at allYou, but with recourse — bond claim, small claims, credit reporting

These need different defences. The first three are prevented at booking. The fourth is prevented by credit checking and mitigated by the surety bond.

Vetting a broker in fifteen minutes

Run this every time, on every new counterparty. The value comes from it being a routine and not a judgement call — judgement is what gets bypassed when the load is good and the phone is ringing.

  1. Get the MC number in writing. Every legitimate US broker has FMCSA-issued authority. A counterparty who will not put the MC number in an email is the end of the conversation.
  2. Look it up on SAFER at safer.fmcsa.dot.gov. Check three things: operating status reads AUTHORIZED, the operation classification includes Broker, and the legal name and address match what you were sent — exactly, not approximately.
  3. Call the number on SAFER, not the number in the email. This single step defeats most identity-theft attempts, because the impostor controls the email and the phone in the signature but not the number FMCSA has on file. Ask for the person by name and confirm the load exists.
  4. Check the email domain against the company's real website. Impersonation typically inserts a word — "logistics", "usa", "inc" — or changes the top-level domain. A broker corresponding from a free webmail address for a six-figure book of business is telling you something.
  5. Check the bond and note the surety. The bond is on the SAFER record. Write down which surety company holds it — that is who you claim against later, and it takes ten seconds now and an afternoon later.
  6. Check payment history. SAFER shows authority, not behaviour. A broker can be fully AUTHORIZED and a chronic 90-day payer. Credit services in this market run roughly $20–$50 a month and pay for themselves on the first avoided invoice.

The one that catches the most

Step 3. Call the SAFER number. Not the one on the rate confirmation, not the one in the signature block, not the one the person gives you when you ask. The number FMCSA has on file.

Reading a SAFER record properly

Four fields decide whether to continue, and three of them are routinely skimmed:

FieldWhat good looks likeWhat should stop you
Operating statusAUTHORIZEDAnything else — including "not authorized" for the classification you need
Operation classificationBroker listedCarrier-only authority arranging freight — that is the definition of unauthorised brokering
MCS-150 / authority dateEstablished historyAuthority under 90 days old, paired with high rates and urgency
Legal name and addressMatches the paperwork exactlyA different address, a different DBA, a mailbox-service address

The second row is the one that matters most for double brokering, and it is the one nobody looks at. A company with motor carrier authority and no broker authority is not permitted to arrange your load for someone else. If they are doing it anyway, the money chain behind you has no legal basis and you are last in line.

The bond, and what changed in January 2026

Every US broker must maintain $75,000 of financial security — a surety bond (BMC-84) or a trust fund (BMC-85). If a broker fails to pay you, you file a claim against it.

The FMCSA broker and freight forwarder financial responsibility rule took effect 16 January 2026, implementing provisions Congress mandated in MAP-21 back in 2012. What it changed:

  • Immediate suspension authority. If a broker's available financial security falls below $75,000 and is not replenished within seven calendar days, FMCSA may suspend the operating authority.
  • Surety notification duty. The surety or trustee must notify FMCSA of a broker's financial failure or insolvency, rather than the market finding out through unpaid carriers.
  • Narrower eligible assets. Acceptable assets are limited to cash, irrevocable letters of credit from federally insured depository institutions, and US Treasury bonds.
  • Loan and finance companies are no longer eligible to act as BMC-85 trustees.
  • Three-year ineligibility plus monetary penalties for violating entities.

The bond's real limitation

$75,000 is the total for all claimants, not per carrier. When a broker fails owing $600,000 across forty carriers, the bond covers roughly 12.5 cents on the dollar and the claimants share it.

Which means: file early. The bond is a queue, and a carrier who files the week they notice is in a very different position from one who files three months later.

Your right to see the transaction record

Under 49 CFR 371.3, a broker must keep a record of each transaction it arranges, and any party to that transaction may review it — including what the shipper paid.

The practical catch is that many broker–carrier agreements contain a clause waiving that right, and courts have generally enforced those waivers. So the regulation exists and the contract you signed may have given it away.

Two things follow. First, read the waiver clause before signing a broker packet — it is usually one sentence and it is usually negotiable with smaller brokers. Second, be aware that FMCSA has a supplemental rulemaking pending that would require electronic records provided within 48 hours of request and make waivers harder to enforce. As of this writing it has not been published or made effective — treat it as pending, not as law.

Red flags, ranked by how much they actually predict

Not all warning signs are equal. Roughly in order of how strongly each one predicts a problem:

  1. The rate is far above market for the lane. 50–100% above is not a lucky day. It is the cost of getting you to skip steps 2 through 6, and it is the most reliable single signal there is.
  2. Urgency plus resistance to verification. "The truck needs to be there in two hours" combined with any friction about the MC number, a call-back, or a signed confirmation.
  3. Contact details that do not match SAFER. Different phone, different address, free-webmail domain, or a domain registered recently.
  4. Authority under 90 days old. Legitimate on its own — everyone starts somewhere — but it removes your ability to check history, so it raises the bar on everything else.
  5. Pressure to send the rate confirmation back before details are complete. Blank fields get filled in later, and not by you.
  6. Payment terms that change after the load is booked.
  7. Requests to change payment remittance details by email. Always verify by phone to the known number. This is one of the most common and most expensive attacks in every industry, not just freight.

Protecting yourself after you have accepted

  • Signed rate confirmation before dispatch, always. With the MC number, the legal name, the full rate, the accessorial terms and the payment terms on it. A verbal agreement is not evidence in a bond claim.
  • Never let anyone else's paperwork name your truck. If the load number, shipper reference or carrier name on the documents does not match who hired you, stop and ask before you load.
  • Photograph the seal, the trailer number and the BOL at pickup and delivery. Timestamped, on the driver's phone. Five seconds, and it is the difference between a claim you win and one you argue about.
  • Get in and out times signed. Same photographs, same reason — see the detention guide.
  • Invoice the same day with a complete packet. Speed is protection: the sooner you invoice, the sooner a payment problem surfaces while you still have leverage and while the bond queue is short.
  • Watch for the second broker. If the load was double brokered, a different company's name will usually appear somewhere — on the BOL, on the shipper's gate paperwork, in the tracking request. That is the moment to make a phone call, not after delivery.

What to do when it goes wrong

  1. Document everything immediately. Rate confirmation, BOL, POD, every email and text, call log, the SAFER record as it looked when you booked it. Screenshots with dates.
  2. Demand payment in writing with a specific deadline, sent to the legal address on the SAFER record as well as by email.
  3. File the bond claim. Against the surety named on the SAFER record. Do not wait for the demand letter to fail — a bond is a queue and there is no advantage to being polite about your position in it.
  4. File a complaint with FMCSA through the National Consumer Complaint Database. It does not recover your money directly, but complaint volume is what drives enforcement and what puts a repeat offender's authority at risk.
  5. Report it to the credit services you subscribe to, so the next carrier gets the warning you did not.
  6. Small claims or an attorney depending on the amount. For unauthorised brokering there may be additional claims available; that is a conversation with a lawyer in the relevant jurisdiction, not something to conclude from a web page.

What a fraud event costs

Beyond the invoice, so the fifteen minutes can be priced properly:

Unpaid line haul on one load$2,800
Fuel and driver pay already spent on it$1,470
Administrative time — documentation, claim, follow-up (12 h)$420
Bond recovery, typical partial+$350
Net loss on one event$4,340
Loads at 15% margin required to replace it≈ 10

Ten clean loads to undo one bad one. Fifteen minutes of verification, run on every new counterparty for a year, is perhaps twenty hours. The arithmetic is not close.

Keep the record that makes a claim easy

Log every load with the broker, MC number, rate and payment date. When something goes wrong, the evidence already exists — and the pattern of who pays slowly shows up long before somebody stops paying entirely.
