Factoring, and the price of forty days

Factoring at 3% against a 30-day payer is a 39% annual cost of capital — and it can still be the right decision. Here is how to tell which case you are in.

Factoring is not expensive or cheap in the abstract. It is a price for time, and whether it is worth paying depends entirely on what you would do with the money and what it costs you not to have it. Both of those are numbers.

The problem factoring solves

Freight is delivered today and paid for in thirty to forty-five days. Fuel is paid for at the pump. The driver is paid on Friday. The gap between those two facts is the single largest operational constraint on a small carrier, and it has nothing to do with profitability.

A carrier can be earning a 22% margin and still be unable to take the next load, because the money from the last four is sitting in a broker's payables. Factoring closes that gap for a fee.

Factoring does not make you more profitable. It makes you able to run more loads at the same profitability, and it costs a fixed slice of each one. Those two effects compete.

How it works, mechanically

  1. You deliver the load and submit the invoice plus the signed bill of lading to the factor.
  2. The factor buys the invoice — it is a sale, not a loan — and advances a percentage of the face value, commonly 90–100%, usually within 24 hours.
  3. The broker or shipper is notified to pay the factor directly. This is a notice of assignment, and it is not optional.
  4. When the customer pays, the factor releases any reserve it held back, minus the fee.

Two things follow that carriers should understand before signing. The factor now has a relationship with your customer, and the factor will credit-check your customers — which is a genuine service, since a broker the factor refuses to fund is usually a broker you should not be hauling for.

What it costs in 2026

Published market ranges for truckload carriers:

Carrier profileTypical rate
Established carrier, strong broker mix, volume1.0% – 1.5%
Owner-operator factoring reliable brokers1.5% – 3.0%
New authority, thin credit history3.0% – 5.0%

On a $2,400 invoice at 3%, the fee is $72. That is the number everyone quotes, and on its own it is meaningless, because it says nothing about how long the money was borrowed for.

The number that makes it comparable

To compare factoring against any other use of money, convert the fee into an annualised rate.

effective_apr = (fee_pct ÷ (100 − fee_pct)) × (365 ÷ days_saved)

days_saved = normal_payment_days − factoring_days

Read that table honestly. Factoring at 3% against a 30-day payer is a 39% annual cost of capital. That is more expensive than almost any bank product and comparable to a credit card.

And it can still be the right decision — because the comparison is not against a bank loan you cannot get. It is against not running the load at all.

The comparison that actually matters

If factoring a $2,400 invoice for $72 lets you take a load that nets $520 you otherwise could not have funded, the 39% APR is irrelevant. You paid $72 to earn $520.

If you have the cash anyway and factor out of habit, you are paying $72 for nothing, forty times a month, which is $2,880 a month of pure loss.

Same product. Same rate. Opposite verdict. The variable is whether the cash was actually binding.

Recourse, non-recourse and what you are really buying

RecourseNon-recourse
If the customer never paysYou buy the invoice backThe factor absorbs it
RateLowerHigher — the difference is the premium
Credit checksAdvisoryBinding — they choose who you can haul for

The important caveat: non-recourse usually covers credit risk only — the customer becoming insolvent. It typically does not cover a disputed invoice, a damage claim, a late or missing POD, or a rate disagreement. Those are the ways carriers actually fail to get paid, and they remain yours in both structures.

Read the definition of the covered event in the contract. If it says "insolvency" and not "non-payment", you are buying a narrower product than the name suggests.

Quick pay: the same trade, without the contract

Most large brokers offer quick pay: payment in 1–7 days instead of 30–45, for a discount of typically 2–5% of the line haul.

FactoringQuick pay
Cost1–5%2–5%
ContractUsually 12–24 monthsNone — per invoice
Applies toAll customersOnly that broker
Credit protectionAvailableNone
Available when you need it mostYesNot necessarily

The practical pattern that works for most small carriers: use quick pay selectively on the loads where cash is actually binding, and factor only if that is most of them. Quick pay used four times a month costs a few hundred dollars and no commitment. Factoring everything to solve a problem that occurs four times a month costs several thousand.

When it is worth it and when it is not

The decision reduces to one question, and it is not about the rate.

Is cash actually the constraint?

Yes — factor. You are turning down loads, delaying fuel purchases, paying drivers late, or running the truck under-utilised because the money has not arrived. The fee buys throughput and the throughput is worth more.

No — do not. You have working capital, the truck is as full as you can keep it, and factoring would simply move 2–3% of every invoice to a third party in exchange for receiving money slightly earlier than you need it.

Two tests worth running before you sign anything:

  1. Model the trough. Build the twelve-week cash schedule from the authority guide. If the trough never goes below what you hold in reserve, cash is not your constraint and factoring is a pure cost.
  2. Price it against the marginal load. Take your typical net per load. If the factoring fee across a month is less than the net on the loads it lets you add, it pays for itself. If you cannot name those additional loads, it does not.

And one thing to do regardless of the decision: fix your invoicing speed first. A carrier who invoices the same day with a clean, complete packet is paid materially faster than one who invoices weekly with missing PODs — for free. It is common to find a week of the delay is self-inflicted, and a week of delay at 3% factoring is worth about a quarter of the fee.
