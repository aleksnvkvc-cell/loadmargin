Hours of service, in plain English

Four clocks run at once and you are legal only while all four are satisfied. What each one is, what resets it, and how to check a load against them before you accept.

A load that only works by running illegal was never a profitable load. It was a liability with a rate attached. This is the US property-carrying hours-of-service framework in plain English, and how to check a run against it before you accept.

Why this belongs in a profitability tool

Most carriers treat hours of service as a compliance topic — something the ELD handles and the safety manager worries about. That framing costs money.

The clock is a capacity constraint. A driver has 11 hours of driving and 70 hours of duty in eight days, and those hours are the scarce resource your business actually sells. A load that consumes an extra shift for the same rate is a worse load, whatever the rate per mile says.

And a violation is not a fine. It is a CSA score movement, which moves your insurance premium, which moves your cost per mile — on every load you run afterwards.

The four clocks

Four limits run at once. You are legal only while all four are satisfied.

ClockLimitWhat resets it
Driving11 hours10 consecutive hours off duty
On-duty window14 hours10 consecutive hours off duty
Break30 min after 8h drivingTaking the break
Cycle60h / 7 days or 70h / 8 days34 consecutive hours off duty

The one that catches people is the second. It is a window, not a budget of working hours — and it does not stop.

The 11-hour driving limit

After 10 consecutive hours off duty, a driver may drive a maximum of 11 hours. Then another 10 consecutive hours off before driving again.

Only actual driving counts. Loading, fuelling and waiting at a dock are on-duty time but not driving time — they do not consume this clock, though they do consume the next one.

The 14-hour window

All driving must happen within 14 consecutive hours of coming on duty after a qualifying 10-hour break.

The 14-hour clock does not pause

This is the rule that most often turns a workable load into an illegal one. Sitting at a receiver for four hours does not stop the window — it just consumes four hours of it. Come on duty at 06:00 and you cannot drive after 20:00, whatever happened in between.

Which is why detention is both a cost and a compliance problem. Four unpaid hours at a dock can cost you the second half of your driving day.

The 30-minute break

After 8 cumulative hours of driving time without at least a 30-minute interruption, a driver must take 30 consecutive minutes before driving again.

Since the September 2020 revision, that break can be satisfied by off-duty, sleeper berth, or on-duty-not-driving time — so a 30-minute fuel stop or a wait at a shipper counts. It no longer has to be off-duty.

The 60/70-hour cycle

The rolling limit on total on-duty hours:

  • 60 hours in any 7 consecutive days — for carriers not operating every day of the week
  • 70 hours in any 8 consecutive days — for carriers that do

The carrier chooses which applies. It is rolling, not weekly: each day, the hours from eight days ago drop off the back.

This is the clock that quietly runs out. A driver who works 10 hours a day, six days a week, is at 60 hours by day six and cannot legally start day eight without a restart.

The 34-hour restart

Taking 34 consecutive hours off duty resets the cycle clock to zero.

There is no limit on how often it can be used, and it does not need to include any particular time of day. That flexibility matters for planning: 34 hours starting Friday evening puts a driver back at a full 70 by Sunday morning.

Sleeper berth splits

The required 10 hours off can be split, which gives a driver real flexibility around an awkward appointment:

  • 7/3 split — at least 7 consecutive hours in the sleeper berth, plus a separate period of at least 3 hours off duty or in the sleeper
  • 8/2 split — at least 8 consecutive hours in the sleeper, plus at least 2 hours off duty or in the sleeper

Neither period counts against the 14-hour window, which is the point. Used well, a split lets a driver wait out a bad delivery window without burning the day.

Two exceptions worth knowing

Adverse driving conditions

When conditions that could not have been known before departure arise — weather, an accident closing a road — the driving limit extends from 11 to 13 hours and the window from 14 to 16 hours.

"Could not have been known before departure" does the work in that sentence. A forecast blizzard is not adverse driving conditions; a road closed by a crash in front of you is.

Short-haul exception

Drivers operating within a 150 air-mile radius of their reporting location, and returning to it within the workday, can be exempt from the ELD requirement and from keeping records of duty status, subject to conditions.

Planning a load against the clock

Before accepting, work through five numbers:

  1. Driving time needed. Total miles ÷ realistic average speed. Use 50–55 mph, not the speed limit — that average includes fuel stops, traffic and scales.
  2. Driving time available today. The smallest of: driving left, window left, cycle left.
  3. Is a break due? If the run needs more driving than remains before the 8-hour mark, add 30 minutes.
  4. Extra shifts. Anything beyond today's available driving needs another shift and 10 hours of rest in between.
  5. Compare against the appointment. Total elapsed time versus hours until delivery. If elapsed exceeds the appointment window, the load only works by breaking a rule.

The cycle is the one that surprises people

A 970-mile run needs about 18.7 driving hours at 52 mph. That is two shifts — fine on the daily clocks. But if the driver has only 12 hours left in the 70-hour cycle, the load is impossible without a 34-hour restart first, and no amount of daily planning fixes it.

What a violation actually costs

The fine is the smallest part.

  • The fine itself — real, but survivable
  • CSA score — hours-of-service violations carry weight in the Safety Management System, and the score is visible to brokers and insurers
  • Insurance premium — the score feeds renewal pricing, and insurance is one of the largest fixed costs in your cost per mile
  • Freight access — some shippers screen carriers on safety score before they will tender a load
  • Liability exposure — an out-of-hours driver in an accident changes the legal position entirely

Which is the connection back to profitability: a violation raises your fixed cost per mile through the insurance line, on every load you run for the next year. A load that causes one was never worth its rate.
