Insurance, and the year before the quote

After fuel and the driver, it is your largest cost — and almost everything that sets the price was decided months before you started shopping.

After fuel and the driver, insurance is the largest cost a small carrier carries — and unlike the other two, most of what determines it was decided months before the quote. By the time you are shopping, the price is largely already set.

Why it is the second-largest line

On a single truck with its own authority, insurance is typically 70–80% of first-year startup spend and stays the biggest fixed cost after the truck payment. It does not scale with miles — it accrues whether the truck moves or not — which makes it a fixed cost and puts it directly into your cost per mile through the fixed line.

Fuel you can influence week to week. Insurance you influence over a year, through things that have nothing to do with buying insurance.

The two most commonly misunderstood

Cargo insurance does not cover the load unconditionally. Policies exclude categories — unattended vehicle theft is the classic one, along with refrigeration breakdown unless you buy reefer breakdown cover specifically. Read the exclusions before you haul electronics or pharmaceuticals.

Non-trucking liability is not the same as bobtail. One is defined by whether you are on dispatch, the other by whether a trailer is attached. Carriers assume they are covered driving home empty on a Sunday and discover the distinction afterwards.

The one that surprises people

A lapse in coverage is a major red flag on the next quote — often worse than a minor at-fault claim. Letting a policy cancel for non-payment to save two months costs far more than two months at renewal, and some markets will not quote you at all afterwards. If cash is tight, talk to the agent before the lapse, not after.

The first-year penalty, and how long it lasts

Under twelve months of authority you are in the most expensive bracket that exists, and it is not negotiable. Underwriters have loss data on new authorities and it is bad. This is not personal and no amount of explaining your driving history moves it much.

What does move: the CDL holder's own record. An experienced driver with a clean MVR opening a new authority pays materially less than a new CDL opening one. If you have a clean twenty-year record, make sure the underwriter has it.

The bracket improves sharply at the one-year mark and again at two years, provided the record stays clean. Budget the first year at the high end and treat the second-year drop as the reward for surviving.

Deductibles: the trap in the obvious saving

Raising the deductible lowers the premium immediately, which makes it the first thing everyone reaches for. It is also the decision most likely to end a small carrier.

A $2,500 or $5,000 deductible is a bill that arrives on the worst possible day — during the same week the truck is not earning, on top of a repair, in a slow month. If your reserve cannot absorb it without touching fuel money, the lower premium is not a saving. It is a bet.

A rule that holds up

Take the higher deductible only if the difference between it and the lower one is already sitting in an account you do not touch. Not "will be" — is. Otherwise take the lower deductible and treat the extra premium as what it is: the price of not gambling.

Nine ways to lower it that actually work

  1. Start quoting 60+ days before renewal. Rushed quotes get conservative pricing, and filing delays at the last minute create gaps.
  2. Check your PSP and MVR for errors before the underwriter does. Violations recorded against the wrong carrier or driver are common and disputable — see DataQs.
  3. Fit a forward-facing camera and say so. Many markets discount for it, and in a disputed accident it is the difference between an at-fault claim and a closed file.
  4. Classify accurately. Overstating your radius or misdescribing commodities costs money every year and can void a claim.
  5. Correct the garaging address. Where the truck sleeps changes theft and collision rating. A rural yard is not a downtown street.
  6. Do not carry limits you do not need. If no broker asks for $250,000 cargo, $100,000 is the cheaper honest answer.
  7. Keep continuous cover. Never let a policy lapse, for any reason.
  8. Fix the CSA score. The slowest lever and the largest one — crossing back under a threshold is worth 30–50%.
  9. Use an agent who works trucking. A generalist places you in whatever market answers first. A specialist knows which underwriter likes your radius and commodity.

Notice how few of these happen at renewal. Almost every real saving is earned in the twelve months before the quote.

Working a renewal properly

  1. Sixty days out: pull your own MVR, PSP and CSA record. Dispute errors now — corrections take weeks.
  2. Forty-five days out: give your agent an updated loss run, mileage, radius, commodity mix and any safety equipment fitted. Incomplete information gets priced pessimistically.
  3. Thirty days out: compare quotes on the same limits and deductibles. A cheaper quote with a $5,000 deductible and $50,000 cargo is not cheaper.
  4. Read the exclusions, especially cargo. Unattended vehicle theft and reefer breakdown are the two that matter most.
  5. Never let the old policy end before the new one starts. Even a one-day gap is a lapse.

Putting it in your cost per mile

Insurance is a fixed cost. It belongs in monthly overhead, divided by total monthly miles — including empty ones.

annual premium ÷ 12 = monthly insurance

insurance_cpm = monthly insurance ÷ monthly total miles

At $16,000 a year and 9,800 total miles a month, that is $1,333 a month and 13.6 cents a mile — every mile, loaded or empty, moving or parked.

Which reframes the CSA question usefully: a 40% premium increase is not "$6,400 a year". It is 5.4 cents a mile added to your break-even, on every load you quote for the next twelve months.
