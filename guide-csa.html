CSA scores, and what they really cost

It is not a safety rating — it is a ranking against carriers your size. And 92% of trucking underwriters price your insurance off it.

A CSA score is not a safety rating. It is a percentile — a ranking against carriers your size — and almost every consequence that follows from it is financial rather than regulatory. Ninety-two per cent of commercial trucking underwriters price your insurance off it.

What a CSA score actually is

Compliance, Safety, Accountability is FMCSA's programme for identifying carriers worth investigating. The scoring engine behind it is the Safety Measurement System, and what it produces is not a grade out of a hundred. It is a percentile.

A score of 80 in Vehicle Maintenance does not mean you passed 80% of inspections. It means 80% of carriers your size have a better maintenance record than you do. You can improve every month and still see the number rise, because everyone else improved faster.

You are not being measured against a standard. You are being ranked against your competitors — which means the score moves when they change, not only when you do.

The seven BASICs

Each is scored separately. A carrier can be clean in six and in trouble in one.

BASICWhat feeds it
Unsafe DrivingMoving violations found at roadside — speeding, following too close, seat belt, phone use
Crash IndicatorState-reported crashes involving a fatality, an injury, or any vehicle towed away
Hours of ServiceLog and ELD violations, form-and-manner errors, driving beyond the limits
Vehicle MaintenanceEquipment defects — brakes, tyres, lights, load securement
Controlled Substances / AlcoholPositive tests, refusals, programme violations
Hazardous MaterialsHM placarding, packaging and paperwork violations
Driver FitnessLicensing, medical certification and qualification-file violations

Two of these behave differently from the rest. Crash Indicator counts crashes regardless of who was at fault — a car turning into your trailer still counts. And Hazardous Materials only applies if you run placarded freight, which is a reason some carriers stay out of it entirely.

The thresholds that trigger intervention

Cross one and FMCSA starts paying attention — warning letters first, then investigation.

BASICThresholdWhy it is set there
Unsafe Driving65thStrongest statistical link to future crashes
Hours of Service65thSame
Crash Indicator60thLowest threshold of the general set
Hazardous Materials60thConsequence of failure is higher
Vehicle Maintenance80thVery common violations, so the bar is higher
Controlled Substances / Alcohol80thRare events
Driver Fitness80thMostly paperwork

Passenger carriers face tighter thresholds, starting around the 50th percentile. For freight, the two at 65 are the ones to watch — they are the categories a dispatcher's decisions actually influence.

Severity weighting: not all violations cost the same

Every violation carries a severity weight from 1 to 10. This is where carriers misjudge risk badly — a stack of trivial write-ups is not the same as one serious one.

ViolationSeverity
No fire extinguisher1
Tyre tread depth3
Inoperative brakes8
Operating under a disqualified CDL10
Positive drug test10

One brake violation weighs as much as roughly eight missing fire extinguishers. The practical consequence: a pre-trip that checks brakes and tyres properly is worth more than one that checks everything superficially.

Time weighting, and why last month hurts three times as much

Recency multiplies. The same violation counts for three times as much in its first six months as it will in its second year.

Age of violationMultiplier
0 – 6 months3×
6 – 12 months2×
12 – 24 months1×
Over 24 monthsdrops off entirely

Two things follow from this, and both are useful

A bad score is temporary. Everything you did more than two years ago is gone. A carrier who cleans up genuinely will see the number fall on its own — not instantly, but reliably, and fastest between months six and twelve.

Clean inspections help, but not by cancelling anything. They add inspections with no violations to your denominator, which dilutes the rate. Volume of clean inspections is the only lever that works quickly, and it is why carriers who avoid scales are usually making their score worse, not better.

The part nobody explains: you are ranked against peers

Carriers are grouped by inspection volume, then ranked within the group. That has consequences that feel unfair until you see the mechanism.

  • A small carrier's score is volatile. With eight inspections on record, one violation moves the percentile enormously. A 400-truck fleet absorbs the same violation without noticing.
  • Moving between peer groups changes your score without any change in behaviour. Grow past a group boundary and you are suddenly compared to a different set.
  • You need a minimum number of inspections before a BASIC is scored at all. A brand-new carrier has no percentile in most categories, which is not the same as a good one — brokers and insurers read a blank differently from a clean record.

What a bad score costs in money

The regulatory consequence is a warning letter, then possibly an investigation. The financial consequence arrives much faster and hits much harder.

Where it bitesEffect
Insurance premiumsApproaching a threshold: 10–30% increase. Above it: 30–50%+
Broker accessMany brokers screen on CSA and simply stop offering you freight — usually without telling you why
Shipper contractsDirect contracts routinely set a CSA ceiling as a condition
Inspection frequencyA high score makes you more likely to be pulled in, which creates more chances to score again

Put a number on the insurance effect. On the illustrative owner-operator premium of about $16,000 a year, crossing a threshold at a 40% increase is $6,400 a year — roughly what two decent loads a month earn in net profit.

The compounding trap

A high score means more inspections. More inspections mean more chances to find something. More findings mean a higher score. Carriers describe this as being targeted; it is really just the loop closing on itself. The only exit is a run of clean inspections, which is why hiding from scales makes it worse.

Surviving the inspection itself

Most roadside violations are found in a handful of places, and nearly all of them are visible at a pre-trip if the pre-trip is real.

  1. Brakes. Out-of-adjustment slack adjusters, air leaks, worn linings. Severity 8, and the single most common serious finding.
  2. Tyres. Tread depth, sidewall damage, flat or audibly leaking, mismatched duals.
  3. Lights and reflectors. Trivially cheap to fix, and a large share of write-ups.
  4. Load securement. Straps, chains, working load limits, edge protection.
  5. Paperwork. Medical card, annual inspection, registration, IFTA decals, ELD instruction sheet and blank logs in the cab.

Two behavioural points matter more than any checklist. The driver's demeanour changes the depth of the inspection — calm, organised and cooperative shortens it. And a clean inspection is worth having on record, so pulling into an open scale with a truck you are confident in is doing your score a favour, not risking it.

Fixing a score that is already high

In order of how quickly each one moves the number:

  1. Stop the bleeding in the top BASIC. Work out which category is over threshold and what is actually driving it. It is usually one repeated violation type, not a spread.
  2. Accumulate clean inspections. The fastest lever available. Run legal, run inspection-ready, and go through open scales.
  3. Fix the maintenance programme, not the last truck. If brakes appear twice, the interval is wrong, not the mechanic.
  4. Wait out the multipliers. At six months a violation drops from 3× to 2×. At twelve, to 1×. At twenty-four it is gone. That happens whether or not you do anything, and it is why panic is rarely warranted.
  5. Deal with the driver, honestly. If one driver generates most of the violations, no maintenance policy fixes the score.

Disputing a violation

Violations can be challenged through FMCSA's DataQs system. It is worth using — and worth understanding what it can and cannot do.

Worth disputing: factual errors (wrong USDOT number, wrong driver, wrong vehicle, duplicate entries), violations later dismissed in court, and crashes that were plainly not your fault where the report says otherwise.

Not worth disputing: a violation that was correctly recorded because it was correctly found. A dispute is not an appeal against the rule.

Submit with evidence — repair receipts, court disposition, photographs, the inspection report number. Vague disagreement gets closed. Check your record regularly rather than annually: errors are easier to correct while the paperwork still exists, and the 3× multiplier means an error in its first six months is costing you the most.
