The Clearinghouse, and the query due every January

This is the compliance area where a paperwork mistake takes your licence rather than your money. Ten minutes a year, in the first week of January, is most of it.

This is the compliance area where a paperwork mistake takes your licence rather than your money. An owner-operator who never tests positive can still end up with a downgraded CDL by missing a query that takes ten minutes a year.

What the Clearinghouse is

The FMCSA Drug and Alcohol Clearinghouse is a federal database of drug and alcohol programme violations by CDL and CLP holders. It exists because the previous system relied on drivers disclosing their own history and on carriers chasing previous employers who often did not reply.

It records positive tests, refusals, and the steps back to compliance. Employers must query it before hiring and once a year for every driver they employ. Registration is free at clearinghouse.fmcsa.dot.gov.

If you are an owner-operator, read this first

You are both the employer and the driver, and the rule anticipates exactly that:

You cannot administer your own testing programme

An owner-operator must be enrolled in a consortium / third-party administrator — a C/TPA — and must designate that C/TPA in the Clearinghouse to query and report on their behalf. You may not select yourself for random testing, and you may not run your own programme.

Expect roughly $50–$150 a year for consortium membership, plus the cost of the tests themselves.

This catches people who got their own authority and assumed the testing obligation only applied to fleets. It applies from the first day you operate under your own authority with a CDL.

The two kinds of query, and the January date

QueryWhenWhat it returns
Full queryBefore hiring any driverFull violation detail. Requires the driver's specific electronic consent in the Clearinghouse
Limited queryAt least annually for every employed driverOnly whether information exists. If it does, a full query must follow within 24 hours
The annual query for the previous year is due by 5 January. It is the single most-missed obligation in this whole area, and it is missed by people with nothing to hide.

As an owner-operator, that means querying yourself — through your C/TPA — every year, and keeping the record for three years. It takes minutes. Put it in the calendar for the first week of January along with your other annual filings.

The six tests, and what is in the panel

TestTrigger
Pre-employmentBefore performing any safety-sensitive function. Drug test required
RandomUnannounced selection through the consortium, spread across the year
Post-accidentWhere the accident meets the regulatory criteria — a fatality, or an injury treated away from the scene or a vehicle towed away combined with a citation
Reasonable suspicionBased on a trained supervisor's specific, contemporaneous observation
Return to dutyAfter a violation, once the SAP process is complete. Directly observed
Follow-upUnannounced, on the SAP's schedule, after returning to duty. Directly observed

The DOT drug panel tests for marijuana, cocaine, amphetamines, opioids and PCP.

Marijuana, in a state where it is legal

State law is irrelevant here. Marijuana remains prohibited under federal DOT testing rules regardless of state legality, and a medical recommendation is not a valid explanation to the Medical Review Officer. Drivers have lost their careers on this exact misunderstanding.

CBD products are the trap underneath it. They are not regulated for consistency, several have tested well above their labelled THC content, and "it was CBD" is not a defence to a positive.

Random selection, and why it is not random to you

FMCSA sets the minimum annual random testing rates and publishes them each year — historically 50% of the average driver population for drugs and 10% for alcohol, with the drug rate having moved between 25% and 50% depending on the industry-wide positive rate. Check the current year's rate; do not assume last year's.

Selection must be genuinely random and spread reasonably through the year. Practically, for a one-truck operation:

  • You may be selected more than once in a year, or not at all. Both are normal.
  • When selected, you must report immediately — the consortium will give you a window, and delay is treated as evasion.
  • Keep a collection site you can actually reach on route. Being 400 miles from home when selected is the usual complication, and every C/TPA can direct you to a local site.

Clearinghouse II: the CDL downgrade

Since 18 November 2024, the consequences moved from employment to licensing.

State driver licensing agencies now query the Clearinghouse when a CDL or CLP is issued, renewed, transferred or upgraded — and they receive notification when a driver's status changes.

A driver in "prohibited" status has their commercial driving privilege downgraded by the state. The CDL is removed from the licence until the return-to-duty process is complete.

What changed in practice: before, a prohibited driver could not legally be employed but the licence sat in the wallet. Now the licence itself goes. Getting it back means completing the process below and going back to the state to have the commercial privilege restored.

What counts as a violation

  • A verified positive drug test
  • An alcohol test of 0.04 or higher
  • A refusal to test — see below, this is broader than it sounds
  • Actual knowledge of prohibited use — an employer observing use, an admission, a traffic citation for driving under the influence in a commercial vehicle
  • Reporting for duty or remaining on duty while using alcohol within four hours of performing a safety-sensitive function

Return to duty: the road back, and how long it is

A violation is not permanent, but the process is longer and more expensive than most people expect, and every step is mandatory and in order:

  1. Removal from safety-sensitive functions immediately. No driving, at all.
  2. Evaluation by a Substance Abuse Professional — a qualified SAP listed by DOT. You choose and pay for it.
  3. Complete the education or treatment the SAP prescribes. The SAP decides what it is; you do not negotiate it.
  4. Follow-up SAP evaluation confirming you have complied.
  5. Return-to-duty test, directly observed, with a negative result. An employer must arrange it — an owner-operator arranges it through the C/TPA.
  6. Follow-up testing programme — a minimum of six unannounced tests in the first twelve months, and the SAP may extend the programme up to 60 months.

The realistic cost and timeline

SAP evaluation, prescribed treatment, the return-to-duty test and up to five years of follow-up testing typically run to several thousand dollars, and the minimum time out of the seat is measured in weeks even in the fastest case.

Nobody can shorten it, and any service promising to remove a violation from the Clearinghouse is selling something that does not exist. Records stay for five years from the violation or until the return-to-duty process is complete, whichever is later.

Refusals, which are treated as positives

A refusal has the same consequence as a positive test, and the definition is wider than "saying no":

  • Failing to appear for a test within a reasonable time after being directed
  • Leaving the collection site before the process is complete
  • Failing to provide a sufficient sample without a valid medical explanation (shy bladder has a defined procedure — follow it rather than walking out)
  • Failing to permit a directly observed collection where one is required
  • A sample that is adulterated or substituted
  • Failing to cooperate with any part of the collection process

The most common accidental refusal is the first one: being told to report and not going quickly enough. Treat a selection notice as immediate.

Penalties

Civil penalties of up to $16,000 per violation apply to employers and drivers who fail to comply with Clearinghouse requirements — including failing to run the queries, failing to report violations, and employing a prohibited driver.

The penalty amounts are adjusted for inflation annually, so check the current figure. The point stands regardless: a missed annual query is not a paperwork nuisance, it is an enforcement matter, and there is nothing to gain by getting it wrong.

Under $300 a year for a one-truck operation, and it belongs in fixed overhead alongside your permits and filings — it is charged whether the truck moves or not. See cost per mile for where that lands in the rate you need.

The annual checklist

  1. Registered in the Clearinghouse, as both employer and driver if you run your own authority
  2. C/TPA designated in the Clearinghouse to act on your behalf
  3. Consortium membership current and paid
  4. Annual query run by 5 January for the previous year, and the confirmation saved
  5. Query records kept three years
  6. Contact details current in the Clearinghouse, so a selection notice actually reaches you
  7. A collection site identified in the areas you run, before you need one

Compliance is fixed cost, not a surprise

Consortium fees, permits, filings and insurance run every month regardless of miles. Put the real annual total into the overhead field and let it price itself into every load.
