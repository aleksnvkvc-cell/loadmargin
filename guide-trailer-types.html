Dry van, reefer or flatbed — choosing on the real numbers

Reefer pays more per mile and costs more to run. Flatbed pays well and asks more of you. Dry van is the easy default that everyone competes in. The headline rate is the worst way to choose — here is the comparison that matters.

Guide Updated 8 September 2026 10 min read

New carriers pick a trailer by the rate per mile they see posted, which is exactly backwards. Reefer posts the highest rate and can net the least; flatbed asks for skill and pays for it; dry van is easy and crowded. The right choice is the one where your cost, your skill and the freight you can actually find line up.

Why the headline rate misleads

A higher rate per mile that comes with a more expensive trailer, more expensive maintenance, higher claim risk and more deadhead is not automatically more money. The number that matters is what nets to you per mile after the costs the trailer type imposes — and those costs differ enormously.

Rate per mile ranks trailer types in one order. Net per mile, after the costs each type carries, frequently ranks them in a different one.

Dry van: the default

An enclosed box. The most common trailer, the most freight, the lowest barrier to entry — and therefore the most competition and the lowest rates.

  • Pros: cheapest trailer to buy and maintain, easiest freight to find, no load securement skill, low claim risk on general freight
  • Cons: the most competitive rates, because anyone can do it

Dry van is the sensible default for a first truck: the freight is everywhere, the skill bar is low, and you can learn the business without the trailer type fighting you.

Reefer: higher rate, higher everything

A refrigerated van. Hauls food, produce and pharma — freight that never stops moving — at a higher rate per mile. The higher rate is real; so is everything behind it.

What the higher reefer rate is paying for

  • A reefer unit that burns its own fuel — a running cost dry van does not have at all
  • A far more expensive trailer to buy and to maintain, with a mechanical unit that fails
  • The highest claim risk of the three — a temperature excursion can ruin an entire load, and reefer breakdown is often a separate insurance endorsement
  • Tighter appointments and more detention at produce houses and DCs

Run cheaply and reliably, reefer can be the best net of the three. Run with an old unit and thin records, a single spoiled load erases months of the rate premium.

Flatbed: skill as a moat

An open deck — steel, lumber, machinery, building materials. Pays well, precisely because it asks more of the driver and fewer people can do it well.

  • Pros: strong rates, less competition because of the skill barrier, freight tied to construction and industry
  • Cons: load securement is on you — straps, chains, tarps, and the physical work and liability that come with them; weather exposure; a real injury risk; and freight that softens when construction does

The securement skill is the moat: it keeps rates up because it keeps entrants out. It is also a genuine responsibility — a shifted or lost load on an open deck is a serious matter.

Read down the columns, not across one row. The trailer that wins is the one whose costs and skill demands you can actually carry — then the rate is yours to keep, not to spend on the trailer.

Claim risk by trailer type

Claim exposure differs sharply, and it flows straight into your insurance and your cargo-claim risk:

  • Dry van — lowest, mostly theft and handling damage
  • Reefer — highest, because temperature failure can total a load, and the cover often needs a specific endorsement and a working temperature download
  • Flatbed — securement and weather; a lost or shifted load is on you

Which to start with

For a first truck, dry van is the usual honest answer: cheap to enter, freight everywhere, and the lowest way to make the beginner mistakes that every new carrier makes. Move to reefer or flatbed once you understand your cost per mile and can price the extra costs each carries — not because the posted rate looked bigger.

Price the trailer's costs before the rate

Put the reefer fuel, the securement time, the claim risk into your cost per mile first. Then the rate tells you the truth about which freight actually pays.
