The rate confirmation, line by line

The rate con is not a formality — it is the contract, and it is where detention, fees and deductions are decided before the wheels turn. Here is how to read one, and the clauses worth a phone call before you sign.

The rate confirmation — the “rate con” — is the contract for a single load. Sign it and you have agreed to its terms, including the ones that decide whether you get paid for detention, who eats a fee, and what can be deducted. Reading it takes two minutes and prevents most of the disputes carriers have.

What a rate confirmation actually is

When you book a load, the broker sends a rate con: the agreed rate, the stops, the reference numbers, and the terms. You sign and return it. That signature is the agreement — not the phone call, not the friendly voice, the document. If the two disagree later, the document wins.

“But he said on the phone” is worth nothing against a signed rate con that says otherwise. Get the phone promise onto the document before you sign.

The lines that matter

  • Rate — the total, and whether it is all-in or has a separate fuel surcharge. Confirm it matches what you agreed.
  • Pickup and delivery — dates, times, and whether appointments are firm or windows. Late here is a real problem.
  • Weight and commodity — so you are legal and correctly equipped.
  • Reference / PO numbers — you will need them to get paid; a missing one can hold an invoice.
  • Broker MC and contact — check the authority is active before you haul.

Where detention and accessorials live

This is the part carriers skip and then regret. The rate con should state:

  • Detention — when it starts (usually after two free hours), the hourly rate, and the cap. If it is silent, you have agreed to no detention.
  • Layover, TONU (truck ordered not used), lumper fees, extra stops — who pays and how much.
  • Reefer set point and mode for temperature-controlled freight, and who specified it — this decides a claim later.

If it is not on the rate con, assume it is not owed

Detention you did not get written into the document is detention you will fight for and often lose. The time to add “detention $X/hr after 2 hours” is before you sign, by asking the broker to put it on. Most will. See the detention guide.

The clauses that move money away from you

Read the fine print for terms that quietly shift risk or cost:

  • Fines or fees passed to the carrier — late fees, rejection fees, and penalties that can wipe out the load
  • Chargebacks and offsets — language letting the broker deduct a claim from this or other loads; read it before you accept a deduction later
  • Quick-pay terms — the discount for fast payment, and the standard days if you wait
  • Double-brokering prohibitions — your own promise not to re-broker, which is fine, but note the penalties

Making the rate con match reality

If a term is wrong or missing, fix it before signing:

  1. Call the broker and ask for the change in writing — a corrected rate con.
  2. Do not just cross it out and sign; get a clean amended document.
  3. If the broker will not put a promised term in writing, treat that as information about the broker.

Keep it — it is your evidence

The signed rate con, the bill of lading and your timestamps are what you invoice against and what you use if payment is short or detention is disputed. Keep every one, matched to its load. A factoring company will want it; so will you, if it goes wrong.

A 60-second checklist

Rate correct, and all-in vs fuel surcharge clear✓
Pickup / delivery times you can actually make✓
Detention terms present (start, rate, cap)✓
Reference / PO numbers included✓
Broker MC active, contact correct✓
No fee or chargeback clause you did not agree to✓

The rate con sets the real rate

Detention terms, fuel surcharge and fees on the rate con change what the load actually pays. Put the real all-in figure into the calculator, not the headline rate.
