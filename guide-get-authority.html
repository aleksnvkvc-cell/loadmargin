Getting your authority, step by step

The filings that turn an idea into a carrier, in the order they actually happen — and an honest flag on the parts of federal registration that are changing in 2026 and worth confirming directly before you act.

Getting authority is a sequence, not a single form, and the steps depend on each other — insurance cannot file until the number exists, the number does not activate until insurance and process agents are on file. Do them out of order and you wait. Here is the order.

The map: what you are actually applying for

Three different things get called "authority" loosely:

  • A USDOT number — your safety identity, required to operate a commercial vehicle interstate at all
  • Operating authority — permission to haul for hire, historically shown as an MC number
  • The surrounding filings — BOC-3, insurance, UCR, IRP, 2290 — that make the authority usable

You need all of it before you can legally take a paid load under your own name.

A 2026 caveat, stated plainly

Federal registration is modernising, and sources disagree on the details

FMCSA has been consolidating registration under its Unified Registration System, and reporting in 2026 differs on how MC numbers and the old separate application fee are being handled — some sources describe MC-style docket numbers being retired in favour of USDOT-only identification, others still describe the traditional MC number and a $300 application fee.

Because this is genuinely in transition, this guide does not state the current fee or process as settled fact. Before you file, confirm the exact steps and cost directly on fmcsa.dot.gov or with a reputable filing service. What follows is the shape of the process, which has not changed even as the paperwork does.

Step 1: USDOT number and operating authority

You apply through FMCSA's registration system. You will need your business formed first (an EIN, and usually an LLC — see the business structure guide), your details, and your operation type. The system issues the USDOT number and starts the operating-authority application in the same flow.

Whatever the current fee, the application itself is the small part of the cost. What follows is where the money is.

Step 2: BOC-3 process agents

A BOC-3 designates a process agent — someone who can receive legal documents on your behalf — in every state you operate. You do not file it yourself; a process-agent service files it for you, typically for a modest one-time fee, and covers all states at once.

Authority will not activate without it, so this is done early, not last.

Step 3: insurance and the filings

This is the real gate and the real cost. For most for-hire property carriers, federal minimum public liability is $750,000, and in practice you carry $1,000,000 because that is what brokers require. Your insurer files proof with FMCSA electronically — the BMC-91 (public liability) and, where required, cargo filings.

The application fee is pocket change next to insurance. A new authority pays far more for cover in year one, because there is no record to price against — see the insurance guide.

Two things to know: the filing is done by your insurer, not you, and the authority clock does not really start until those filings post.

Step 4: the waiting period and activation

New for-hire operating authority carries a protest / vetting period before it goes active — historically around three to four weeks after the application, during which the insurance and BOC-3 filings must land. Your authority shows as active only when all of it lines up.

Plan for a gap of several weeks between applying and legally hauling. Carriers who line up a truck payment before the authority is active spend that gap paying for a truck that cannot earn.

Step 5: the filings that come right after

Active authority is not the finish line. Before or just after your first load:

  • IRP (apportioned plates) through your base state — the plate that lets you run interstate, billed by mileage across states
  • IFTA — the fuel-tax account and decals; see the IFTA guide
  • UCR — the annual fee; see the UCR guide
  • HVUT / Form 2290 — the heavy-vehicle tax and the stamped Schedule 1 you need for the plate; see the 2290 guide
  • Drug & alcohol programme — a consortium and Clearinghouse registration; see the Clearinghouse guide

And then: the new entrant audit

Every new carrier enters an 18-month monitoring period with a safety audit in the first 12 months. It is a documentation test, and most new carriers who fail it fail on empty folders, not unsafe driving. Build the four files from day one — the new entrant audit guide is the checklist.

What it costs, honestly

The FMCSA application is the cheap part. The real first-year total — insurance, plates, permits, filings, and the working-capital gap before invoices pay — runs to many thousands. The authority cost guide breaks the full number down; treat the application fee as a rounding error against it.

Budget the gap, not just the fees

The expensive part of a new authority is the weeks with a truck and no active authority, and the month before your first invoice pays. Put both in the plan.
