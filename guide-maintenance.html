Maintenance is a reserve, not an invoice

Eleven quiet months and then a $9,400 week. A cost model built on recent invoices is wrong in both directions — and most confidently wrong right before a major repair.

Maintenance is the line that makes cost models lie. Not because it is large, but because it arrives in lumps: eleven quiet months and then a $9,400 week. A model built on last month's invoices is wrong in both directions, and it is wrong most confidently right before a major repair.

Why maintenance breaks cost models

Fuel is smooth. Driver pay is smooth. Insurance is a monthly number. Maintenance is not — it is a sequence of small routine costs punctuated by occasional large ones, and the large ones dominate the total.

A carrier who models maintenance from recent invoices does one of two things. In a quiet stretch they under-cost every load, quote too cheaply, and appear more profitable than they are. In the month of a major repair they over-cost every load, refuse rates they should have taken, and conclude the lane went bad.

You do not have a maintenance expense. You have a maintenance reserve that you occasionally spend. Model the reserve; the invoices are just withdrawals.

The reserve, not the invoice

The correct treatment is to charge every mile a fixed amount and accumulate it, exactly as you would if you were setting money aside — because you should be.

maintenance_cpm = (12-month total repair + tyre + PM spend) ÷ 12-month miles

monthly_reserve = maintenance_cpm × miles_this_month

Two rules make this work:

  1. Use twelve months, minimum. Anything shorter is dominated by whether a major item happened to fall inside the window. Twenty-four months is better once you have it.
  2. Include everything. Tyres, oil services, DOT inspections, brakes, the breakdown that ended in a tow, the trailer. Excluding "unusual" items guarantees the number is too low, because unusual items are most of the total.

If you genuinely have no history, the commonly used starting figures are $0.15 to $0.25 per mile for a well-maintained truck of moderate age, running higher for older equipment and for vocational work. Start at the top of that band rather than the bottom — a reserve that turns out to be too large is a pleasant problem.

Roughly 19.6 cents per mile. That is the figure that belongs in the cost model every single month, including the eleven months when almost nothing broke.

Sanity check on the reserve

At 19.6 cents on 118,000 miles a year, the reserve accumulates $1,923 a month. If your actual cash balance never gets near that, the reserve is fiction and the next big repair will be financed by a credit card at a rate that makes factoring look cheap.

The in-frame is the item that decides whether a cost model was honest. At $24,000 over 1,000,000 miles it is 2.4 cents per mile that must be reserved from day one, on a truck that will not need it for six years. Carriers who do not reserve it discover the number at the worst possible moment, and typically trade the truck rather than fix it — which is the same money with a worse outcome.

Tyres deserve their own line

Tyres are the one large maintenance item that is genuinely predictable, which makes them the best place to prove the reserve method works.

Four cents a mile, every mile, forever. Add casing value if you retread and the number falls; run them past the wear bars and it rises, because a roadside blowout costs a service call, a tyre at emergency pricing, and half a day.

The cheapest maintenance there is

Correct inflation pressure affects tyre life, fuel economy and blowout risk simultaneously. It costs nothing and takes five minutes at a pre-trip. It is the highest-return maintenance action available to a small carrier, and it is the one most consistently skipped.

How the number moves with truck age

Maintenance cost per mile is not constant across a truck's life. A rough shape, useful for planning rather than as a benchmark:

Truck ageIndicative maint. CPMWhat dominates
0 – 2 years / under 300k$0.08 – $0.12PM and tyres; most failures under warranty
3 – 5 years / 300–700k$0.15 – $0.22Aftertreatment, brakes, first big-ticket items
6 – 8 years / 700k – 1.1M$0.22 – $0.35Overhaul territory, drivetrain, rising downtime
9+ years$0.30 – $0.50+Highly variable; downtime often exceeds parts

This is the real content of the buy-versus-keep decision, and it is usually argued with the wrong comparison. The right one is not "payment versus repair bill". It is:

keep: maintenance_cpm + downtime_cpm

replace: payment_cpm + newer_maintenance_cpm + downtime_cpm

An older truck with 30 cents of maintenance and 6 cents of downtime is costing 36 cents a mile. A newer truck at 40 cents of payment and 10 cents of maintenance costs 50. The old truck is cheaper — right up until the downtime figure moves, and downtime is the variable that moves fastest and gets measured least.

The cost that is not the repair

A three-day breakdown is not a $2,400 repair. It is a $2,400 repair plus three days of a truck that earns nothing and costs its fixed rate regardless.

Repair invoice$2,400
Tow$650
Fixed cost, 3 days at $195.77$587.31
Lost contribution, 3 days × 550 miles × $0.75$1,237.50
Driver retained / hotel / repositioning$420
True cost of the event$5,294.81

The invoice was 45% of it. This is why preventive maintenance economics are so lopsided, and why "we fixed it cheap" is frequently the more expensive outcome.

The breakdown frequencies here are illustrative, not measured — the point is the shape. Doubling PM spend to halve or better the breakdown rate wins by a wide margin, because a PM service costs $320 and a breakdown costs $5,295. You do not need the frequencies to be precise for the conclusion to hold; you need the ratio to be roughly right, and it is.

The practical version: a PM schedule tied to mileage, an inspection habit at every pre-trip, and a rule that a small noise gets looked at at the yard rather than at 2 a.m. on a shoulder.

Tracking it so the number stays honest

Four fields per event, and the number maintains itself:

  • Date and odometer — lets you compute intervals and per-mile cost properly.
  • Category — PM, tyres, brakes, aftertreatment, engine, drivetrain, trailer, roadside. Eight buckets is plenty and it is enough to see where the money goes.
  • Planned or unplanned — the single most informative field, because the ratio between them tells you whether your PM programme is working.
  • Downtime hours — the cost nobody records and the one that decides buy-versus-keep.

Recompute the twelve-month rolling cost per mile monthly and feed it straight into the cost model. When it moves, you find out from your own data rather than from a surprise, and the load calculator is telling the truth again the same day.
