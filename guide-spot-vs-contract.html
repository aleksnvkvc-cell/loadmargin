Spot vs contract, the two markets you sell into

Every load is priced in one of two markets: the spot market, where rates swing with the week, or contract freight, locked for months. They move against each other, and knowing which you are in — and when — is how you avoid selling cheap at the bottom.

Freight is priced in two markets, and most owner-operators live entirely in one without naming it. The spot market is today's price for today's load; contract freight is a rate locked for months. They behave differently, they move against each other, and knowing which you are selling into changes how you price and when you commit.

The two markets

The same lane can pay two different rates depending on which market the load comes from. Understanding the difference is the difference between reacting to rates and reading them.

Spot and contract are not two kinds of load. They are two ways of pricing the same freight — one by the week, one by the season — and they rarely agree.

The spot market: freedom and volatility

Spot is the load-board market: one-off loads priced by today's supply and demand. It offers freedom — you take what you want, when you want, and in a hot market spot rates spike above contract. It also carries volatility — in a soft market spot collapses, sometimes below your cost, and you feel every swing. Spot is where a nimble carrier wins in good times and bleeds in bad ones.

Contract freight: stability and its price

Contract freight is a committed rate over a period — steadier, more predictable, easier to plan and finance around. The price of that stability is that you are locked in: when spot spikes, your contract rate does not, and you watch others earn more. When spot collapses, though, your contract holds — and that is when it earns its keep. Contract trades upside for a floor.

How they move against each other

The cruel timing of the freight cycle

Spot and contract move on different clocks. Spot reacts immediately; contract lags. In a hot market, spot runs above contract, and carriers rush to grab spot — just as shippers push to lock cheap contracts for later. When the market turns, spot craters below contract, and the carriers who locked good contracts are protected while the all-spot carriers scramble. Then it reverses. The trap is always chasing the market you are in now as if it will last.

What a small carrier actually does

A one-truck operation rarely lands big direct contracts on day one, so most start almost entirely on spot. That is fine — but the goal is to build toward a mix:

  • A base of steadier, repeatable freight — a few brokers or a direct shipper who give you regular lanes — for a floor
  • Spot to fill the gaps and to catch the upside when the market runs

The mix is the point. All spot is a rollercoaster; all contract can leave money on the table. A floor of steady freight plus spot on top is how a small carrier gets both a predictable base and the upside.

Timing, and not getting caught out

You cannot forecast the market, but you can avoid its classic mistakes: do not commit your whole capacity to cheap freight at the bottom just because you are scared, and do not assume a hot spot market will still be hot next quarter. Keep your walk-away number fixed to your costs, not to the market's mood, and let it — not fear or greed — decide what you haul.

Your cost sets the floor, not the market

In either market, the load either beats your cost per mile or it does not. Keep the number fixed and let it decide.
