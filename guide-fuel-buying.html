Buying fuel, net of tax

Drivers cross a state line to save eleven cents a gallon and lose money on the trip. The sign is not lying — it is answering a different question from the one you are asking.

Fuel is the largest variable cost in trucking and the one most often optimised backwards. Drivers cross a state line to save eleven cents a gallon and lose money on the trip, because the number on the sign is not the number they pay.

The pump price is not the price

Diesel at the pump includes state fuel tax. Under IFTA, the tax you owe is based on where you drove, not where you bought — so tax paid at the pump in one state becomes a credit against tax owed everywhere you ran.

Which means the tax portion washes out. What you actually paid for the diesel is the pump price minus the tax that will come back to you.

Comparing pump prices across state lines is comparing two numbers that each contain a different tax. The sign is not lying — it is answering a different question.

Net-of-tax: the only comparable number

The comparison you want is one line:

net price = pump price − state fuel tax per gallon

State diesel tax rates vary enormously — from near zero to well over thirty cents a gallon, and they change quarterly. Any comparison that ignores a thirty-cent spread while chasing an eleven-cent one is going to be wrong more often than it is right.

Two conditions before this is worth doing

You must actually be filing IFTA. The credit only exists if you claim it. If you are running intrastate and not on IFTA, the pump price is your price and none of this applies.

The current quarter's rates. Rates are republished every quarter. Working from last year's table produces confident, wrong answers — use the current IFTA rate sheet or a tool that pulls it.

A worked example that reverses the answer

Three stops on the same run, illustrative numbers:

StopPump priceState taxNet price
State A$3.65$0.29$3.36
State B$3.55$0.12$3.43
State C$3.60$0.17$3.43

State B has the cheapest sign by ten cents. State A is the cheapest fuel by seven. On a 150-gallon fill that is $10.50 in the wrong direction, on a decision that felt obviously right at the time.

Do this every fill, twice a week, fifty weeks, and the arithmetic gets serious.

Retail-minus, cost-plus, and which you actually have

Fuel card programmes come in two shapes and they behave differently:

TypeWhat it meansBehaviour
Retail minusA discount off the posted cash priceLooks large at expensive stops. The stop sets the base, so a high posted price can eat the discount
Cost plusThe stop's cost of the fuel, plus a fixed marginNarrower headline, far more predictable, and usually better where you have volume

A "50 cents off" retail-minus deal at a stop posting 40 cents above the local average is a ten-cent saving. Compare programmes on the final price paid at the stops you actually use, not on the advertised discount.

  • Check whether the discount applies to the cash or the credit posted price
  • Check the transaction fee per fill — a fixed fee is a real per-gallon cost on small buys
  • Check whether the discount is capped by gallons per transaction or per month
  • Check the network. A brilliant rate at stops that are not on your lanes is not a rate

The out-of-route mile that eats the saving

The most common self-inflicted loss on this page. Before detouring for cheap fuel:

saving = gallons × price difference

cost = extra miles × your cost per mile

At a cost per mile of $1.85 — see cost per mile for how to get your real number — a twelve-mile detour costs $22.20. A 120-gallon fill at seven cents better saves $8.40.

You lost $13.80 and you feel like you saved money, because the saving is visible on the receipt and the cost is not visible anywhere.

The rule of thumb, and why it is a bad rule

A common shortcut is "never detour more than five miles for fuel". It is roughly right at a typical fill size and typical cost per mile, and it is exactly the kind of borrowed number this site exists to argue with.

Work out your own break-even once: breakeven miles = (gallons × price difference) ÷ cost per mile. Write it on a card. It changes only when your cost per mile does.

Splitting the buy

You do not have to fill in one place. Two useful patterns:

  • Buy enough to reach the cheap state, then fill there. Fifty gallons at the expensive stop to get you across, then a full fill on the net-cheapest fuel on the route.
  • Never fill full immediately before scaling. Fuel is weight — roughly 7 lb a gallon, so 200 gallons is about 1,400 lb, mostly on the steer and drives. On a heavy load that is the difference between legal and an overweight ticket.

The counterweight to both: every extra stop costs time, and time on a 14-hour clock is not free. Two fuel stops in a day where one would do is twenty minutes you did not have on a tight delivery.

The cheapest gallon is the one you do not burn

Buying better moves the price by a few cents. Burning less moves the quantity, and the quantity is the bigger lever.

ChangeTypical effect
Speed 68 mph instead of 62 mphmeaningfully worse mpg — aerodynamic drag rises with the square of speed
Tyres 10 psi under pressuremeasurably worse mpg, and shorter tyre life
Trailer gap and skirtsan aerodynamic gain on highway miles
Progressive shifting and steady throttlethe largest driver-controlled variable there is

Run the arithmetic on your own truck rather than trusting a percentage. At 100,000 miles a year and $3.60 diesel, going from 6.5 to 7.0 mpg saves about $3,956 — more than almost any fuel card deal on offer, and it costs nothing but discipline.

This is also why the peg mpg in a fuel surcharge matters so much; the fuel surcharge guide works that through.

Idling, in cents per mile

An idling truck burns roughly 0.8 gallons an hour and travels nowhere. At $3.60 that is $2.88 an hour.

6 hours a night, 5 nights a week, 48 weeks1,440 hours
At 0.8 gal/hr1,152 gallons
At $3.60$4,147 a year
Spread over 110,000 miles3.8 cents per mile

Idling also accrues engine hours, which pulls forward every service interval — so the number above understates it. An APU or a bunk heater is a capital decision you can now actually evaluate, because you have the annual figure to compare it against.

A weekly fuel routine

  1. Plan the fills with the load, not at the low-fuel light. The light is where bad decisions get made.
  2. Compare net of tax, using the current quarter's rates.
  3. Check the detour against your break-even miles before turning off.
  4. Keep every receipt — gallons, date, state, and the odometer. This is your IFTA filing and your audit defence in one.
  5. Reconcile monthly: gallons bought against miles run gives your real mpg. It will differ from the dash readout.
  6. Put the real mpg into your cost per mile, and recheck it every quarter. A quarter-mpg drift is a maintenance signal before it is a fuel signal.

How this interacts with the fuel surcharge

Fuel surcharge is calculated from a published index and a peg — it does not care where you bought. So every cent you save on the buy, and every tenth of an mpg you gain, is kept in full. It does not reduce your surcharge.

That is the whole reason this is worth the attention: fuel buying is one of the few places in freight where the saving is entirely yours and nobody negotiates it away from you.
