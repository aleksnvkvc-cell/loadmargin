Health insurance, the benefit nobody hands you now

Go independent and the health coverage a company quietly paid for becomes your bill — one many owner-operators leave out of their rate entirely. Here is how to think about it, where coverage comes from, and the deduction that softens it.

When you drove for a company, someone quietly paid a large share of your health insurance. Go independent and that whole cost lands on you — and it is one of the most commonly forgotten numbers when a new owner-operator sets a rate. It is not optional spending; it is part of the cost of running yourself as a business.

Why this belongs in a freight guide

Because it changes the rate you need. A company driver's take-home came after the employer's benefit contribution; an owner-operator's rate has to cover it out of gross. Leave health insurance out of your cost per mile and you have quietly given yourself a pay cut you did not notice — the classic mistake in the lease-purchase and go-independent decision.

The company job's real pay included benefits you never saw as a line. Independence does not remove that cost — it just moves it onto your invoice, where you have to price it in.

Put it in your cost per mile

Take your monthly premium (plus a realistic share of out-of-pocket costs), and divide by your monthly miles, and you have health insurance in cents per mile. It sits in fixed overhead with insurance and the truck payment. Small per mile, large per year, and invisible until you cost it — which is exactly why so many drivers underprice.

Where coverage comes from when self-employed

Common routes for a self-employed driver in the US:

  • The individual marketplace — plans bought directly, with subsidies possible depending on income
  • A spouse's employer plan — often the cheapest option if it is available to you
  • Association or group plans — some trucking associations (see associations) offer access to group coverage
  • Short-term or catastrophic-style plans — cheaper premiums, much thinner cover; understand the gaps before relying on one

The self-employed health-insurance deduction

A deduction that gives some of the premium back

Self-employed people can generally deduct health-insurance premiums for themselves and their family, within limits and conditions, which lowers the after-tax cost of coverage. It is one of the more valuable deductions available to an owner-operator, and exactly the kind of thing to confirm with an accountant so you actually claim it. Do not leave it on the table.

High-deductible plans and HSAs

A high-deductible health plan paired with a Health Savings Account (HSA) can lower premiums and let you save pre-tax money for medical costs — useful for a generally-healthy driver who wants a lower monthly bill and a tax-advantaged cushion. The trade-off is a bigger bill if you actually need care, so it suits someone who can carry that deductible. Weigh premium against worst-case, not just the monthly number.

Choosing without overpaying

  1. Check a spouse's plan first — often the best value if available.
  2. Compare marketplace plans on total expected cost — premium plus likely out-of-pocket, not premium alone.
  3. Ask your accountant about the deduction and how it changes the real cost.
  4. Match the deductible to your cash cushion — a low premium you cannot back with savings is a gamble, not a saving.
  5. Price the result into your rate. Whatever you choose, it belongs in your number.

It is a real fixed cost

Health insurance is part of the cost of being your own employer. Put it in your cost per mile so your rate actually covers your life, not just your truck.
