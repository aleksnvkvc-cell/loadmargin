Load securement, where the rate premium is earned

Flatbed pays more because securement is skilled, physical and on you. Get it wrong and it is a roadside violation, a lost load, or worse. Here is the working-load-limit math, the aggregate rule, and the checks the regulation requires.

On a flatbed, the load is held on by what you put on it. That is why flatbed pays a premium — and why securement is both a heavily-inspected violation category and a genuine life-safety matter. A lost load on an open deck can kill someone. The rules exist for a reason, and they are worth knowing cold.

Why securement is its own responsibility

A dry van holds cargo by being a box. A flatbed holds it by the driver's straps, chains, binders and judgment. Federal cargo securement rules set minimums, inspectors check them closely, and the consequence of getting it wrong ranges from a violation to a fatality. This is the part of flatbed that keeps rates up because it keeps casual entrants out.

Securement is the one area where a paperwork mistake and a life-safety mistake are the same mistake. Treat it accordingly.

Working load limit: the core number

Every tiedown — strap, chain, binder — has a working load limit (WLL), the amount it is rated to secure. Securement math is built on WLL, not on the breaking strength and not on how tight it feels. The number is marked on the device; a strap with no legible rating is not a rated tiedown.

The aggregate working load limit rule

The aggregate WLL must be at least half the cargo weight

The core rule: the combined (aggregate) working load limit of all the tiedowns must equal at least 50% of the weight of the cargo secured. On a 40,000-lb load that is 20,000 lb of aggregate WLL across your straps and chains. There are also rules on the number of tiedowns based on the length and weight of the article. Get either wrong and you are both out of compliance and genuinely unsafe.

Work it out for the load, do not eyeball it: add up the WLL of every tiedown you are using and check it clears half the cargo weight, then check you have enough tiedowns for the length.

Tiedowns, edge protection and the basics

  • Straps for lighter, softer freight; chains and binders for steel, machinery and heavy equipment
  • Edge protection where a strap crosses a sharp edge — a cut strap is a failed strap
  • Blocking and bracing to stop the load shifting fore-and-aft
  • Dunnage and friction matter, but they supplement tiedowns, they do not replace the WLL math

The checks the law requires

Securement is not just at the start. The rules require you to check the load:

  1. Before driving off — the initial securement
  2. Within the first 50 miles, and re-tighten — loads settle and straps loosen
  3. Periodically as you drive — at defined intervals or when you change duty status

Skipping the 50-mile check is both a violation and how loads come loose — because that is exactly when a settled load slackens its own straps.

Commodity-specific rules

Beyond the general rules, the regulation has specific requirements for certain commodities — logs, coils, pipe, concrete, vehicles, and more — each with its own method because each fails differently. If you haul a specialised commodity, learn its specific rule; the general math is the floor, not the whole answer.

Securement in your cost per mile

Securement is time and equipment, and it belongs in your economics:

  • Time tarping and strapping is unpaid unless you bill it — it eats into the hours-of-service clock
  • Straps, chains, tarps and edge protection wear out and get replaced
  • The claim and injury risk is real — it flows into your insurance

This is the real content of the flatbed rate premium. Price it in, and the premium is earnings; ignore it, and the premium was just paying for the extra work you did not count.

The premium pays for the work — count the work

Securement time, equipment and risk are why flatbed pays more. Put them in your cost per mile so the higher rate is profit, not reimbursement.
