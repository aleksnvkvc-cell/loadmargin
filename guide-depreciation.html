Depreciation, and the deduction that can backfire

A truck is a capital asset you write off over time — or all at once. Section 179 and bonus depreciation let you take a huge deduction now, which sounds obviously good and sometimes is not. Here is how it works and where it bites.

A truck is not an expense you deduct in one line like fuel — it is a capital asset, and the tax code lets you deduct its cost as it wears out, or lets you front-load that deduction dramatically. Front-loading feels like free money and occasionally is the wrong move. Understanding why is worth real dollars.

What depreciation is

When you buy a truck, you cannot deduct the whole price as a normal expense — it is an asset expected to last years. Depreciation is how you deduct that cost over its useful life, a slice a year, matching the deduction to the wear.

Depreciation does not change what the truck cost you. It changes when you get the tax benefit — and timing is the whole game.

Regular depreciation: the default

Under the standard method, a truck is written off over a set recovery period (commercial trucks fall into a multi-year schedule). You take a portion of the cost as a deduction each year until it is fully written off. Steady, predictable, and it spreads the benefit across the years you own it.

Section 179: the whole thing this year

Section 179 lets a business deduct the full purchase price of qualifying equipment — including a truck — in the year you put it in service, up to an annual limit, instead of spreading it out. Buy a $120,000 truck and, if it qualifies and you have the income to absorb it, deduct a large chunk this year.

That is a big deduction, and in a high-income year it can be exactly right. The limit and the rules change year to year, which is the first reason this is an accountant conversation, not a website number.

Bonus depreciation

Bonus depreciation is a related provision that has, in various years, let businesses deduct a large percentage of an asset's cost immediately, sometimes on top of or instead of Section 179. The percentage has changed repeatedly with the law, so the current figure must be confirmed for the year you buy — do not rely on a number you read once.

When taking it all now is a mistake

A deduction is only worth the tax rate you save at

Deducting $120,000 in a year when your profit was low wastes most of it — you cannot save tax you did not owe, and you have used up the deduction you could have spread into future, higher-income years. A big deduction in a lean year can be worth far less than the same deduction spread across good years.

There is also the load-decision angle: a truck fully written off in year one shows no depreciation cost in years two and three, which can make those years look more profitable than they are and lead to bad pricing if you forget the truck is still wearing out.

So the biggest legal deduction is not automatically the smartest one. It depends on your income this year versus your expected income next year — which only you and your accountant know.

Recapture: the deduction that comes back

If you take a large accelerated deduction and then sell the truck for more than its depreciated value, the difference can be recaptured — taxed as income in the year you sell. Owner-operators who Section-179'd a truck and then traded it are sometimes surprised by a tax bill on the sale. It is not a trap exactly, but it is a consequence to plan for.

What to actually ask your accountant

  1. Given my income this year and expected next year, should I take Section 179, bonus, or regular depreciation?
  2. What are the current-year limits and bonus percentage?
  3. How will recapture hit me if I sell or trade in a few years?
  4. How does this interact with my business structure and self-employment tax?

This is one of the clearest cases on the whole site where the right answer is “it depends on your numbers, ask a professional.” The purpose of this guide is to make you ask the right questions, not to give you a figure to act on.

Keep the truck's cost in your cost per mile

However you depreciate it for tax, the truck still wears out. Keep its real cost in your per-mile number so a written-off truck does not fool you into underpricing.
