Winter, and the trip that should not have been made

Winter is where good judgment earns its keep. Chain laws, fuel gelling, the cold-weather pre-trip and the honest truth that the load is never worth the ditch — the season that separates careful operators from expensive lessons.

Guide Updated 8 September 2026 8 min read

Winter turns ordinary miles into the most expensive miles you can run. A slide-off, a gelled fuel system, a frozen brake, or a chain-law violation each cost real money, and the worst of them cost far more than any load pays. The season rewards preparation and punishes the “I had to keep moving” instinct.

Why winter is a business risk, not just weather

An accident on ice is a claim, a repair, downtime and a CSA hit. A gelled truck is a tow and a lost day. A chain-law violation is a fine and a violation. None of it appears on a rate confirmation, and all of it lands on you. Winter is a period where the right business decision is often to slow down or stop — and where the pressure to keep the wheels turning is exactly the trap.

No load pays for a ditch. The math on winter is simple: the most expensive outcome is always worse than sitting, so the bias should be toward caution.

Chain laws

Mountain and northern states have chain laws that become mandatory in conditions. When a chain law is in effect you must have and use the required chains — and:

  • Carry the chains before the pass, not hope to buy them at the base in a storm at triple price
  • Know how to install them — the roadside in a blizzard is a bad classroom
  • A violation for not chaining when required is a fine and a delay, and driving without required chains is how trucks end up jackknifed

Fuel gelling and winter diesel

Diesel gels in the cold, and a gelled truck does not run

In deep cold, the paraffin in diesel can gel and clog the fuel filter, and the truck stops. Defences: fuel with properly winterised (blended) diesel in cold regions, use an anti-gel additive when appropriate, keep the tank fuller (less condensation), and do not let DEF freeze solid where it matters. Prevention is cheap; a tow and a lost day is not.

The cold-weather pre-trip

The pre-trip changes in winter:

  • Brakes and air system — moisture freezes; drain the air tanks
  • Lights and wipers — you will need every one
  • Tyres — tread matters more on snow; check condition
  • Chains stowed and accessible, with the tools to fit them
  • Emergency kit — you may be stopped for hours; carry warmth, water and food

Driving the conditions

Slow down well before you think you need to, increase following distance dramatically, avoid using the engine brake on slick surfaces where it can break traction, and be most careful on bridges and ramps that freeze first. Black ice does not announce itself. The truck that arrives late is worth infinitely more than the one that arrives in a wrecker.

The judgment call

The hardest part is deciding not to drive. A broker's appointment, a dispatcher's pressure, your own need for the miles — none of it outweighs a serious accident. Shutting down for a storm is a professional decision, not a failure, and the carriers who last are the ones who make it. Document the conditions, communicate early, and sit if you must.

Winter risk is in your numbers too

A weather claim or a lost day is real cost. The careful season is the profitable season.
