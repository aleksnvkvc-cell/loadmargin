Cargo claims, and the two minutes at pickup

Carriers lose claims they could have won because nobody wrote two words on a delivery receipt. The structure is fixed, and most of it is decided before the truck moves.

A cargo claim is not a negotiation about fairness. It is a legal process with a fixed structure: the claimant proves three things, and then you either fit one of five defences or you pay. Carriers lose claims they could have won because nobody wrote two words on a delivery receipt.

The law that governs every claim

Interstate cargo liability in the US runs on the Carmack Amendment (49 U.S.C. § 14706), which dates to 1906 and pre-empts most state law claims about damaged freight.

Carmack makes the carrier liable for the actual loss to the goods — near strict liability. Whether you were careless barely enters into it. The trade-off is that the defences and the deadlines are equally rigid, and they work in your favour if you know them.

Carmack is not asking whether you were at fault. It is asking whether the freight arrived in the condition it left in — and if not, whether one of five specific things caused it.

What the claimant has to prove

Before you owe anything, the claimant must establish three elements:

  1. The cargo was given to you in good condition.
  2. It arrived damaged, short, or not at all.
  3. A specific amount of loss.

Only then does the burden shift to you.

Element one is where most claims are actually won

A clean bill of lading — signed by your driver with no exceptions noted — is normally treated as evidence that the freight was in good condition when you took it. You signed for it that way.

Which means the two minutes at pickup matter more than anything you do later. If pallets were already crushed, cases already wet, seals already broken, and the driver noted it, element one fails and the claim usually dies there. If the driver signed clean, you have handed the claimant their first element.

Your five defences

Carmack recognises exactly five. Nothing else works, and "we were careful" is not on the list.

DefenceWhat it coversIn practice
Act of GodNatural events beyond human controlTornado, flood, lightning. Not ordinary bad weather you chose to drive in
Act of the public enemyMilitary action, armed conflictEffectively never applies in domestic freight
Act of the shipperBad packaging, bad loading, bad labellingThe one that matters. Shipper-loaded and sealed, inadequate packaging, mislabelled
Act of public authoritySeizure, quarantine, destruction by governmentAgricultural holds, customs, law-enforcement seizure
Inherent viceThe nature of the goods themselvesProduce that ripens, chemicals that separate, metal that oxidises

Note what is missing: theft is not a defence. Neither is a subcontractor's mistake, an accident that was not your fault, or a mechanical failure. Those are exactly what cargo insurance is for.

"Shipper load and count" is not a magic phrase

Writing SLC or STC on the bill of lading helps — it signals you did not load it and could not verify the count. But it is not automatic protection. It works when you genuinely had no opportunity to observe the loading, and it works far better combined with a photograph of how the trailer was loaded and a note of the seal number.

The clocks, and the one that ends it

EventDeadlineWhose obligation
File a written claim9 months from deliveryClaimant — from the reasonable delivery date if the freight never arrived
Acknowledge the claim30 daysYou

Status update, if still unresolvedevery 60 daysYou
File suit after denial2 yearsClaimant

These are minimums set by regulation; a contract may give the claimant longer but not shorter.

The nine months is your friend and the 30 days is your risk

A claim filed on day 275 is fine. On day 280 it is time-barred. Check the date on every claim before you engage with the substance of it — a late claim needs no defence.

But your own 30-day acknowledgement and 120-day decision are obligations. Ignoring a claim does not make it go away; it makes you look unreasonable in front of whoever decides it later, and it is a regulatory violation in its own right.

Released value: capping your exposure in advance

By default you are liable for the full actual value. A released value agreement caps it — say $2.00 a pound — in exchange for a lower rate.

Courts only enforce it if you did it properly. All of the following must be true:

  • The shipper was offered a genuine choice between full value and released value
  • The choice was made in writing
  • The agreed limitation is on the bill of lading
  • The rates for each option were reasonable and available

A released-value line buried in your terms with no choice offered will not survive a challenge. If you want the protection, build it into the rate confirmation as an explicit option, priced.

The bill of lading is the whole case

Eighteen months later, when a claim is being decided, nobody remembers the load. The document decides it. What should be on it:

  • Piece count and description matching what you actually received
  • Any exception, written at the moment it is observed — not later
  • Seal number at pickup, and confirmation the same seal was intact at delivery
  • SLC / STC where genuinely applicable
  • Temperature setting and mode for reefer freight, and who specified it
  • Signatures with printed names and times at both ends

A signature nobody can read, with no time and no printed name, is worth much less than one with both. This costs nothing and it is skipped constantly.

At pickup: the two minutes that decide a claim a year later

  1. Look at the freight before signing. Crushed corners, water staining, torn shrink wrap, leaning pallets, open cases.
  2. Write exceptions on the BOL before you sign it. "3 cases crushed", "pallet 4 leaning", "packaging torn". Specific and short.
  3. Photograph it. Wide shot of the loaded trailer, close-ups of anything noted. Timestamped, on the driver's phone. This is the single highest-value habit on this page.
  4. Record the seal number and photograph the sealed doors.
  5. If shipper-loaded and you could not observe, write SLC and photograph how it was loaded before the doors close.
  6. For reefer: set point, continuous or cycle, and pre-cool confirmed — all written down, with the setting the shipper specified.

At delivery: how to note an exception properly

A driver who accepts a signature saying "damaged" with no detail has produced a document that helps the claimant and not you.

WeakStrong
"Damaged""2 cases on pallet 6 crushed, top layer only, remainder accepted"
"Short""Received 18 of 20 pallets, seal intact at delivery, seal #4471902"
"Refused""Receiver refused at 14:20, reason given: temperature. Reefer showing 34°F, set point 34°F, continuous"

Photograph the exception before the freight leaves your trailer. Once it is on their dock, proving where the damage happened becomes considerably harder.

Responding to a claim

  1. Check the date first. Over nine months from delivery and it is time-barred.
  2. Acknowledge within 30 days, in writing, even if you intend to deny it.
  3. Pull the file — BOL, POD, photographs, seal record, ELD track, temperature download, driver's notes.
  4. Test the three elements. Was the BOL clean at pickup? Is the damage actually documented at delivery? Have they proven a specific amount, with invoices?
  5. Check the amount claimed. Carmack pays actual loss, normally the wholesale or invoice value — not retail, not lost profit, not the shipper's inconvenience. Claims arrive inflated routinely.
  6. Check for salvage. Damaged goods often have residual value, and it reduces the claim. Ask what happened to the freight.
  7. Identify your defence, if you have one, and say which of the five it is.
  8. Decide within 120 days — pay, deny with reasons, or offer a compromise.

Two things worth knowing about the money

Do not let a broker set off a claim against your unpaid invoices without agreement. Whether they may depends on your contract. Read it before accepting a deduction.

Small claims are often cheaper to pay than to fight — and that is a legitimate commercial decision. Just make it deliberately, having checked the deadline and the amount, rather than by default.

Where your cargo policy fits

Cargo insurance covers your Carmack liability, subject to its own limits and — importantly — its own exclusions. The gaps that catch carriers:

  • Unattended vehicle theft. Excluded or heavily restricted in many policies. This is the most common uncovered loss there is.
  • Reefer breakdown. Usually needs a specific endorsement, and often requires a working temperature download to prove the unit ran.
  • High-value or targeted commodities. Electronics, pharmaceuticals, alcohol, copper — check the schedule before accepting the load, not after.
  • Your deductible. A $2,500 deductible means small claims are entirely yours.
