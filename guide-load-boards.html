Finding loads, without living on the board

The load board is where you start and the worst place to stay. Here is how freight really flows to a small carrier, what the boards do and do not tell you, and how to move from chasing posted loads to being called for them.

Every new carrier starts on a load board and many never leave it, taking whatever is posted at whatever it pays. The board is a useful tool and a terrible master. The carriers who make money use it to start relationships, not to live hand to mouth.

How freight reaches a small carrier

Freight moves through a chain: a shipper has goods, often a broker finds a truck, and a carrier hauls it. As a small carrier you can plug in at two points — take brokered freight, or go direct to shippers. Almost everyone starts with brokered freight because it is the easiest to find.

The board shows you the spot market — today's leftover freight at today's price. The best freight is booked before it ever reaches a public board.

What a load board is, and is not

A load board (the large ones are subscription services) lists available loads with origin, destination, weight, equipment and often a posted rate. It is a marketplace for spot freight — one-off loads, priced by today's supply and demand.

  • It is: a fast way to find a load right now, a read on the market, and a source of first contacts with brokers
  • It is not: the best-priced freight, a relationship, or a substitute for knowing your own cost per mile before you book

Reading a posting like a carrier, not a tourist

The posted rate is the least reliable thing on the screen. Before you call:

  • Work the whole trip against your cost per mile, including the deadhead to get to the pickup — a good rate with 150 empty miles in front of it may not be. See the deadhead guide.
  • Check where it leaves you. A strong load into a dead market means an empty or cheap trip out. Price the round trip, not the leg.
  • Vet the broker before you haul — credit and days-to-pay matter as much as the rate. See vetting brokers.

The broker relationship is the real asset

The first load from a broker is a transaction. The value is what comes after: a broker who learns you show up, communicate, and do not fall apart under a problem will call you directly with freight that never hits the board — at better rates, because they are not fielding twenty calls for it.

How a small carrier becomes a broker's first call

  • Answer the phone and give updates without being chased
  • Do exactly what the rate confirmation says
  • Handle a problem calmly — brokers remember who made a bad day worse and who made it manageable
  • Be reachable and consistent, so booking you is the low-risk choice

Reliability is a competitive advantage precisely because so many carriers lack it.

Shipper-direct: the goal, not the start

Cutting out the broker means the margin the broker took is yours — and a direct shipper who trusts you is steady freight, not spot. It is also harder: shippers want proof you are reliable, properly insured and established, which is exactly what you do not have on day one.

So direct freight is where you grow to, once you have a record, references and the paperwork to look like a real operation. Chasing it on day one usually just wastes the days you should be building that record on brokered freight.

The cheap-freight trap on the board

In a soft market the board fills with loads priced below what they cost to haul. The trap is thinking any revenue beats an empty truck — it does not, if the load loses money once fuel, the driver and your fixed cost per mile are counted.

The discipline is a walk-away number: the rate below which you do not haul, derived from your cost per mile, not from what the board is offering. Some weeks that means sitting — and sitting is occasionally correct. See negotiating with numbers.

A finding-loads routine

  1. Know your cost per mile and your walk-away number before you open the board.
  2. Price the round trip, including deadhead and where it leaves you.
  3. Vet the broker on credit and pay days, not just rate.
  4. Deliver flawlessly and keep the contact. Every good load is a chance to earn a direct call next time.
  5. Slowly build a handful of brokers and shippers who call you — the board becomes a backup, not the whole business.
